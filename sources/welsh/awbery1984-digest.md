## Notation and conventions (as the chapter uses them)

- All forms are phonemic, in `/ /`. Length is `:`; primary stress is `'` before the stressed
  syllable. `*` inside slashes marks a non-occurring form.
- The book prints the older IPA glyph **ɷ** for the vowel of Welsh ⟨w⟩; this digest writes it
  **ʊ**. Northern high central vowels are printed barred-i / barred-ɪ, written **ɨ / ᵻ** here.
- Voiceless *rh* is printed as r-with-ring, written **r̥**.
- Diphthongs are written with a **consonantal glide**: `/aj ɔj əj ʊj/`, `/aw ɛw əw ɪw/` — not
  `/ai ɔi/`. This is a substantive analytic claim of the chapter (pp. 94–96, 101), not just
  notation: Awbery argues diphthongs are *vowel + consonantal glide*, and the glide patterns with
  the sonorants.
- **`O` = obstruent, `S` = sonorant** (p. 86). Clusters are classified `O+O`, `S+S`, `S+O`, `O+S`.
- Figure legend classes **A / B / C** are defined separately for each vowel-length tree.
- "**Final**" in the length trees covers **both stressed finals and monosyllables**;
  "**nonfinal**" covers **penultimates** (p. 70, explicit).

**Note 1 (p. 103) — data provenance.** "Data relating to Pembrokeshire derives from my own
fieldwork, and I am indebted to **Glyn E. Jones and Beth Thomas for information about the
Llanwrtyd area** and the Clwyd valley respectively." Awbery adopts a phonemic framework "for
convenience".

**Note 2 (p. 103) — loanword scope.** "In this discussion we omit consideration of **recent
monosyllabic loans from English as they are not integrated into the native vowel length system,
and obscure its clear patterning**."

## Part 1 — Vowel length

### p. 66 — monosyllables, south Wales

Before a single **sonorant** both long and short vowels occur:

> /gwɪn/ 'white', /prɛn/ 'tree', /man/ 'place', /brɔn/ 'breast', /grʊn/ 'ridge of ploughland'

"Where the following consonant is an obstruent however the vowel must in most cases be long. This
is true of voiced stops, of voiced and voiceless fricatives, and of the voiceless lateral /ɬ/."

> /biːd/ 'world', /ɬeːd/ 'width', /taːd/ 'father', /boːd/ 'to be', /knuːd/ 'crop'
> /priːð/ 'earth', /beːð/ 'grave', /ɬaːð/ 'to kill', /moːð/ 'way'
> /r̥iːχ/ 'furrow', /hweːχ/ 'six', /baːχ/ 'small', /moːχ/ 'pigs', /huːχ/ 'sow'
> /kiːɬ/ 'hazel', /gweːɬ/ 'better', /ɬaːɬ/ 'other', /hoːɬ/ 'whole', /tuːɬ/ 'hole'

"Only before a voiceless stop do we find a short vowel."

> /jɛt/ 'gate', /at/ 'to', /ɬɔk/ 'sheepfold', /krʊt/ 'boy'

"If there is no following consonant at all **only long vowels** are possible."

> /kiː/ 'dog', /ɬeː/ 'place', /daː/ 'good', /toː/ 'roof', /ɬuː/ 'oath'

"And if there is a following cluster **only short vowels** are possible. **The identity of the
consonants making up the cluster is irrelevant**; all that matters is that there should be a
cluster of some kind."

> /pɪnt/ 'pound', /gwɛrθ/ 'value', /plant/ 'children', /gɔlχ/ 'wash', /kʊsk/ 'sleep'

### p. 67 — systematic vs accidental gaps; the /m/ and /ŋ/ problem

Systematic gaps: `/*kɪ/` (short vowel + zero), `/*piːnt/` (long vowel + cluster). "Neither
/\*kɪ/ nor /\*piːnt/ could possibly be an acceptable word in Welsh."
Accidental gaps: /pɪnt/, /pant/, /pɔnt/ exist but there is no /pɛnt/ or /pʊnt/ — "possible but
unexploited slots".

**The /m ŋ/ blur (bears directly on `digest.md` §4.3 row 4).** Short vowels occur freely before
/m/ and /ŋ/:

> /ɬɪm/ 'sharp', /klɛm/ 'idea', /mam/ 'mother', /dɔm/ 'manure', /kʊm/ 'valley'
> /ɪŋ/ 'suffering', /r̥ɛŋ/ 'row', /paŋ/ 'fit', /ɬɔŋ/ 'ship', /mʊŋ/ 'mane'

"Long vowels are a different matter. **Very few long vowels appear before /m/; no long vowels at
all appear before /ŋ/.**" — the sole example given is /biːm/ 'I was'. Awbery declines to decide
whether this is a systematic restriction or an accidental gap: both readings are "unsatisfactory",
and "**Accidental gaps will be ignored in the body of this chapter**". **/m/ and /ŋ/ therefore sit
in class B (LONG OR SHORT) in Fig. 1, not in class C (SHORT).**

### pp. 68–69 — stressed penultimates, south Wales

"In most polysyllables word stress falls on the penultimate syllable, and here the pattern of
restrictions is **very similar** to that described above for monosyllabic forms."

> /'miːnid/ 'minute', /'eːnu/ 'name', /'kaːnol/ 'middle', /'ɬoːnið/ 'quiet', /pat'ruːne/ 'patterns'

> /'ɬiːdu/ 'ashes', /'eːde/ 'thread', /'kaːder/ 'chair', /'koːdi/ 'to lift', /'muːdul/ 'haycock'
> /'biːðe/ 'churn', /'meːðul/ 'to think', /'klaːði/ 'to bury', /di'oːðe/ 'to suffer'

### p. 70 — stressed finals behave like monosyllables

In exceptionally final-stressed words the *penult* is unstressed and short, while the stressed
final "displays the same kind of patterning as we find in other stressed syllables. Before a single
sonorant for instance both long and short vowels may appear, but before zero we find only long
vowels, and before a consonant cluster we find only short vowels."

> /peŋ'gliːn/ 'knee', /ɪwχ'bɛn/ 'above', /maŋ'giː/ 'grandmother', /wiθ'kɔrn/ 'spider's web'

"There is in fact a particularly close link between stressed finals and monosyllables, since
**both require a long vowel before /ɬ/ whereas stressed penultimates require a short vowel** in
this context." — /gweːɬ/ 'better', /əm'heːɬ/ 'far', /'dɪɬad/ 'clothes'

> "We must then take into account three factors in formulating the restrictions on vowel length —
> **stress, the position of the vowel in the word, and what immediately follows it.** Of the three
> stress is basic; an unstressed vowel must be short, while a stressed vowel may be either short or
> long."

> "'Final' covers both **stressed finals and monosyllables**, and 'nonfinal' covers
> **penultimates**."

## Part 2 — The central vowel /ə/

### pp. 76–79

"Only one vowel fails to participate in the complicated patterning described above. This is the
central vowel /ə/ which **has no long equivalent, and is always short** regardless of what follows."

> /'əskavn/ 'light', /'kəvan/ 'whole', /'kənar/ 'early'

Two restrictions, both unique to /ə/:

1. "**The central vowel may appear only in nonfinal syllables.**" Fine in stressed penults,
   unstressed penults and antepenults: /kəm'raːg/ 'Welsh', /kə'tiːn/ 'agreed', /kə'neja/ 'harvest',
   /kə'mɛrjad/ 'character'. "If the syllable is word-final however it is ruled out" — monosyllables
   and final syllables of longer forms alike.
2. "**It may not appear, even in a nonfinal syllable, immediately followed by another vowel.**"
   There is no equivalent of /'rɛːol/ 'rule' with a central vowel in the penult. Awbery explicitly
   rejects deriving this from the length restriction, since /ə/ occurs freely in other environments
   that normally require a long vowel (/'kəvan/, /'rəbið/): "This restriction must then be
   formulated separately."

**Fig. 4  Factors determining if the central vowel is acceptable**

- Is it in a **word-final syllable**?
  - **Yes** → **NOT ACCEPTABLE**
  - **No** → Is it **immediately followed by a vowel**?
    - **Yes** → **NOT ACCEPTABLE**
    - **No** → **ACCEPTABLE**

Exceptions — grammatical items only: /ər, ə/ 'the', /ən/ 'in', /ən/ adj. particle, /ən/ aspect
marker, /də/ 'thy', /və, ən/ 'my'.

**Fig. 5** (south Cardiganshire / north Pembrokeshire): the ban is relaxed for monosyllables
(/bəθ/ 'ever', /bər/ 'short', /mənd/ 'to go'):

- Is it in the word-final syllable **of a polysyllabic form**?
  - **Yes** → **NOT ACCEPTABLE**
  - **No** → immediately followed by a vowel? **Yes** → NOT ACCEPTABLE · **No** → **ACCEPTABLE**

West Pembrokeshire: /ə/ is all but lost, replaced by high vowels — /'kiːvan/ 'whole',
/'buːguθ/ 'to threaten', /'kɪnar/ 'early', /'podri/ 'to rot'.

## Part 3 — Consonants

> Voiceless stops /p, t, k/ · Voiced stops /b, d, g/ · Voiceless fricatives /f, θ, s, ɬ, ʃ, χ, h/ ·

**Medially, between vowels, the full set is possible.**

### pp. 84–85 — Map 5 and south-eastern devoicing

**Map 5  The devoicing of medial stops in south-east Wales.** One isogloss enclosing the
south-east; most of Wales /'eːbol/, south-east /'eːpol/. No place names.

Voiced stops "are banned medially in the word **if they immediately follow the stressed vowel**",
and are replaced by voiceless stops.

| Most of Wales | South-east | |
|---|---|---|
| /'eːbol/ | /'eːpol/ | 'foal' |
| /'kaːder/ | /'kaːter/ | 'chair' |
| /'aːgor/ | /'aːkor/ | 'to open' |

Same replacement if the stop is immediately followed by a nasal or a liquid: /'adnod/ → /'atnod/
'Bible verse', /'eglus/ → /'eklus/ 'church'.

**The length trace survives the merger:** "A medial voiced stop required a preceding long vowel,
and this long vowel is retained even when the voiced stop is replaced by a voiceless one… **The
opposition normally carried by voicing in the stop is therefore now carried by length in the
vowel.**" — /'eːpol/ 'foal' vs /'kapel/ 'chapel'; /'kaːtar/ 'chair' vs /'ateb/; /'aːkor/ vs
/'tekil/.

### p. 85 — /h/

"Turning back to /h/, we find that it is **much more restricted** than the other consonants. It is
basically limited to two contexts."

1. **Word-initial**: /heːn/ 'old', /'haːnes/ 'history'.
2. **Medially if it immediately precedes a stressed vowel, and follows either another vowel or a
   nasal consonant**: /o'hɛrwið/ 'because', /gwa'haːn/ 'apart', /kən'heja/ 'harvest',
   /əŋ'hiːd/ 'together'.

"**In other medial positions and word-finally /h/ is ruled out.**"

## Part 4 — Consonant clusters

### p. 86 — cluster types and the internal constraints

"A wide range of consonant cluster types are possible in Welsh – a sequence of two obstruents, two
sonorants or one of each in either order."

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| O + O | /'ɾagvir/ 'December', /'baχken/ 'boy' |
| S + S | /'gormod/ 'too much', /'marlat/ 'drake', /'kʊmni/ 'company' |
| S + O | /'daŋgos/ 'to show', /'hamðen/ 'leisure', /'ardal/ 'district', /'mʊrθul/ 'hammer' |
| O + S | /'kadno/ 'fox', /'ɛbriɬ/ 'April', /'kəvnod/ 'time', /'kəvlog/ 'salary' |

**Constraint 1 — homorganicity.** "In almost every case **a nasal and a following stop must be
homorganic**."

> /'gʊmpas/ 'around', /'ambeɬ/ 'some', /'kənta/ 'first', /'gʊndun/ 'meadow', /'wɪŋki/ 'weasel',
> /'daŋgos/ 'to show'

"There are occasional exceptions to this rule, such as /'amkan/ 'guess' and /'amgilχ/ 'around', but
they are rare. **There is no similar requirement for a nasal and a following fricative to be
homorganic.**"

**Constraint 2 — voicing agreement in obstruent clusters.** "Obstruent clusters are also
restricted, but in this case **the two consonants must agree in voicing**; either both are voiced
or both are voiceless."

> /'gʊðge/ 'necks', /'ɾagvir/ 'December', /'askurn/ 'bone', /'kɔpsi/ 'top of corn stack'

"**There are no other restrictions on clusters of two consonants.**"

**Constraint 3 — three-consonant clusters.** "Clusters of three consonants however are **highly
constrained. There are two basic types.**"

1. **/s/ + stop + liquid**: /'əsprid/ 'ghost', /'kaskli/ 'to collect'
2. **nasal + stop + liquid**: /'ʃendler/ 'candlestick', /'mentro/ 'to dare'

"**All these constraints on clusters hold throughout Wales without dialectal variation.**"

## Part 5 — Diphthongs

### pp. 90–92 — the sets

South Wales (**Fig. 6**): one set closing towards the front glide /j/ — /aj ɔj əj ʊj/ — and one
towards the back glide /w/ — /aw ɛw əw ɪw/. (Eight, matching `digest.md` §1.)

North Wales (**Fig. 7**) has a larger set: the extra central vowel /ɨ/ gives the extra diphthong
/ɨw/, and the extra glide /ɉ/ (barred *j*) gives a third closing set.

### p. 93 — position; Fig. 8

"**Unlike simple vowels diphthongs are indifferent to what immediately follows**, and may appear
freely in any context." — followed by zero, by a wide range of simple consonants, and by clusters:

> /taj/ 'less', /majn/ 'thin', /gajn/ 'chisel', /gajr/ 'word', /sajl/ 'foundation', /kajb/
> 'mattock', /rajd/ 'need', /krajg/ 'rock', /gwrajð/ 'root', /gwajθ/ 'work', /brajχ/ 'arm',
> /klajs/ 'bruise', /najθ/ 'the one', /majnt/ 'size', /vajŋk/ 'bench'

"On the other hand diphthongs **are** sensitive to their position in the overall word, and… the
difference between final and nonfinal syllables is crucial. Two diphthongs in particular are
affected, and interestingly they react in opposite ways to this distinction."

**/aj/** occurs freely in monosyllables and in final syllables, stressed and unstressed
(/kanja'taj/ 'to permit', /glan'haj/ 'to clean', /'damwajn/ 'accident', /'ɛŋrajft/ 'example'),
"**It does not however appear in nonfinal syllables**, though all the other diphthongs which close
towards /j/ may do so" (/'ejra/ 'snow', /'ɔjri/ 'to get cold', /'blojðin/ 'year').

**Fig. 8  Factors determining if /aj/ is acceptable**

- Is it in a **word-final syllable**?
  - **Yes** → **ACCEPTABLE**
  - **No** → **NOT ACCEPTABLE**

**Note 10 (p. 104):** one exception — /aj/ appears in the prefix /ajl/ 're-', /ajl'nejd/ 're-do';
prefixes are phonologically exceptional in other ways too.

### p. 94 — Fig. 9, and the reanalysis

North also has /aj/ and /aːj/, in monosyllables and final syllables only (/hajl/ 'sun',
/gwaːjd/ 'blood', /kanja'taːj/ 'to permit').

**/əw/** shows the reverse: it "may appear freely in nonfinal syllables, both stressed and
unstressed" (/'təwið/ 'weather', /'kləwed/ 'to hear', /'ɬəwi/ 'to fatten'), but "**may not appear in
monosyllables or in the final syllables of longer forms**, even though other diphthongs closing
towards /w/ may do so" (/ɬiw/ 'colour', /tew/ 'fat', /ɬawn/ 'full', /brown/ 'brown';
/'heːðiw/ 'today', /'ɬɪd'rew/ 'frost', /'joːnawr/ 'January', /pron'hawn/ 'afternoon').

**Fig. 9  Factors determining if /əw/ is acceptable**

- Is it in a **word-final syllable**?
  - **Yes** → **NOT ACCEPTABLE**
  - **No** → **ACCEPTABLE**

"The north and south agree in this."

**Diphthongs reconsidered.** Awbery then argues diphthongs are better analysed as **a vowel followed
by a consonantal glide**. /əw/ = /ə/ + /w/, and /'təwið/ is "directly comparable to /'kənar/ since
in both we have /ə/ in a nonfinal syllable immediately followed by a consonant. **No specific
restriction is needed to account for /əw/**, since it is now merely a special case of the
restrictions on the central vowel /ə/."

### p. 95 — Tables 10–12, the glide restrictions

**Table 10  Restrictions on /ə/ and /əw/**

| | /ə/ | /əw/ |
|---|---|---|
| In nonfinal syllable only | Yes | Yes |
| With following consonant only | Yes | No |

Likewise the gaps `/*ij/`, `/*ow/` (i.e. the missing members of the diphthong sets) fall out of one
symmetrical restriction: "**The glide /j/ may neither precede nor follow a high front vowel, and
the glide /w/ may not precede or follow a high back vowel.** This can be phrased more generally, to
say that **a glide may not stand adjacent to the high vowel which is most closely related to it**."

The same reanalysis explains the missing diphthongs. "Welsh, both in the north and the south, lacks
the diphthongs `/*ɪj/` and `/*ʊw/`. If diphthongs are regarded as unitary phonemes then we have here
merely arbitrary and unrelated gaps in the set." Nor can those gaps otherwise be related to the
ruled-out glide–vowel sequences `/*jiː/, /*jɪ/, /*wuː/, /*wʊ/`.

**Table 11  Symmetrical restrictions on vowels and glides**

| Vowel + Glide | Glide + Vowel |
|---|---|
| \*ɪj | \*jiː , \*jɪ |
| \*ʊw | \*wuː , \*wʊ |

**Table 12  Restrictions on combinations of vowels and glides in the north** — in the north the
restriction extends to the third glide /ɉ/ (barred *j*) and the high central vowels /ɨː ɨ/; there is
no `/*ɨɉ/`, nor the sequences `/*ɉɨː/`, `/*ɉɨ/`:

| | /j/ | /ɉ/ | /w/ |
|---|---|---|---|
| /iː/, /ɪ/ | No | No | Yes |
| /ɨː/, /ɨ/ | No | No | Yes |
| /uː/, /ʊ/ | Yes | Yes | No |

"The distinction between high front and high central vowels and glides is irrelevant to this
restriction. They behave identically to each other in opposition to high back vowels and glides."

### pp. 96–97 — length of the first element; Fig. 10

North: "the first element of a diphthong may be either a long vowel or a short one"
(/gwaːjd/ 'blood', /oːjr/ 'cold', /ɬuːjd/ 'grey' vs /ɬɔj/ 'calves', /hajl/ 'sun', /ɬawn/ 'full').
Only one pair, /aj/ ~ /aːj/, contrasts directly. Two diphthongs (/ew/ and /aw/) are short before a

that the length of the first element depends on what follows, exactly as for simple vowels.)*
"Some consonants required a preceding long vowel, others a short vowel and others again allowed
both long and short. This similarity can be expressed very simply if the diphthongs are analysed as
a sequence of a vowel and a consonantal glide. **It is tempting to align the glides with the
sonorant consonants, the nasals and liquids. Phonetically they are closest to the glides, and they

> /heːn/ 'old', /tiːr/ 'land', /oːjr/ 'cold', /teːw/ 'fat' · /prɛn/ 'tree', /er/ 'although',
> /hajl/ 'sun', /mewn/ 'in'

  - **No** → **SHORT**

    - **No** → **SHORT**
    - **Yes** → Is the glide **/j/, /w/ or /ɉ/**?

## Part 6 — Glides

### pp. 98–99 — position, and Table 13

South Wales has only two glides, **/j/** and **/w/**. "Glides appear freely in initial, medial and
final position as single consonants."

> /jet/ 'gate', /'weːdi/ 'after', /'ɬeja/ 'smallest', /'dewis/ 'to choose', /rɔj/ 'to give',
> /ɬaw/ 'hand'

Two glides may form a cluster: /'drwjol/ 'godly', /'dojwajθ/ 'twice'. "Or a glide may form a

> /'bejdi/ 'cowshed', /'sowdul/ 'heel', /'rejni/ 'those', /'dowlad/ 'loft'
> /'daðjad/ 'date', /'pedwar/ 'four', /'ɔrje/ 'hours', /'ɛnwog/ 'famous'

**Table 13  Glide clusters in different positions in the word**

| | Initial | Medial | Final |
|---|---|---|---|
| G + G | **No** | Yes | **No** |
| G + C | **No** | Yes | Yes |
| C + G | Yes | Yes | **No** |

Word-finally a glide may be followed by a single consonant or a cluster: /krajg/ 'rock',
/kawl/ 'soup'; /bejrð/ 'poets', /awst/ 'August'.
Word-initially the reverse: a glide may be **preceded** by a single consonant — /hweːχ/ 'six',

> /gwlaːd/ 'country', /gwrajð/ 'root', /gwnejd/ 'to do'

`/*gwnejd/`). "**In initial position such clusters must be adjusted, the glide being attracted to a
position between the other two consonants.** No such adjustment is required in final position,
hence the surface asymmetry."

### p. 100 — WHICH consonants may precede a glide initially (Southern)

> "In final position a glide may be followed by any consonant, but in initial position there are
> quite stringent restrictions on what may precede the glide. In **most of south Wales only /h/,
> /k/ and /g/ may precede /w/, and only /d/ may precede /j/.**"

> /hweːχ/ 'six', /gwin/ 'white', /'kwaːrel/ 'window pane', /djawl/ 'devil'

"The **/dj/ cluster is very marginal and is often replaced by the affricate /dʒ/** to give for
instance /dʒawl/."

**North Pembrokeshire** relaxes this: /bjes/ 'I was', /pjaː/ 'belongs to', /djoːd/ 'drink',
/fjoːl/ 'bowl', /hjoːl/ 'farmyard'; /pweːn/ 'pain', /twaːrχ/ 'turfs', /dwaːd/ 'to come',
/kweːd/ 'trees', /fweːs/ 'ditch', /ɬweːr/ 'moon', /lweːθ/ 'again', /nweː/ 'bread-making dish'.
Also glides preceded by a wide range in that area: /krweːn/ 'skin', /klweːd/ 'to hear',
/trweːd/ 'foot'. "**Only in Pembrokeshire is it fair to say that we have a truly symmetrical
relationship between initial and final position.** Elsewhere the relationship is lopsided, and
initial position is more restricted than final."

**North vs south, initial glide clusters:** "where south Wales has the initial cluster **/hw/**,
north Wales has instead **/χw/**. The range of choices is no wider; the two areas vary only in the
identity of the few acceptable initial clusters."

| South | North | |
|---|---|---|
| /hweːχ/ | /χweːχ/ | 'six' |
| /hwajn/ | /χwajn/ | 'fleas' |

"The boundary between north and south for this comes in much the same position as that shown on

The north also has a third glide /ɉ/, which "may not appear alone in initial position nor may it
immediately follow a consonant. It can appear freely alone medially and finally."

### p. 101 — the sonority hierarchy

"Recall that sonorants follow obstruents in initial clusters, and precede them in final clusters.
This gives us a hierarchy with the obstruent regularly appearing next to the word boundary, and the
sonorant between it and the vowel."

> **# O + S + Vowel + S + O #**

"With glides we appear to be adding a third level to this hierarchy of consonantal types. **Any
other consonant, be it an obstruent or a sonorant, appears closer to the word boundary than the
glide**, giving the following pattern:"

> **# O + S + G + Vowel + G + S + O #**

"Note that the final cluster of such forms as /majnt/ 'size' and /bejrð/ 'poets' fits this pattern.
So do the suggested underlying forms of the initial clusters, that is /glw/, /grw/ and /gnw/, though
their modified eventual realizations /gwl/, /gwr/ and /gwn/ do not."

"Glides are on the divide between [consonantal and vocalic]. They are clearly consonantal in many of
the restrictions they undergo, but they have strong links too with the vowels… **Glides alone look
both ways and participate in both consonant and vowel systems.**"

## What this settles

### (a) The cluster inventories, with their conditions

Awbery does **not** give a list of licit clusters. He gives something better for rule-writing: a
**closed set of positional and internal constraints** that generates the list. The complete
statement is:

**Internal (all positions, all dialects — "without dialectal variation", p. 86):**

1. A cluster is any two consonants, of any of the four O/S types. "There are no other restrictions
   on clusters of two consonants" beyond 2 and 3.
2. **A nasal + following stop must be homorganic** (rare exceptions: /'amkan/, /'amgilχ/). No such
   requirement for nasal + fricative.
3. **An obstruent + obstruent cluster must agree in voicing.**
4. **CCC is limited to exactly two shapes: /s/ + stop + liquid, and nasal + stop + liquid.**

**Positional (Table 6):**

| | Initial | Medial | Final |
|---|---|---|---|
| O + O | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| S + S | **No** | Yes | Yes |
| S + O | **No** | Yes | Yes |
| O + S | Yes | Yes | **No (South; Table 9 — all four subtypes)** |

**Word-initial singletons (Table 5):** every consonant except **/χ/, /ð/, /ŋ/** — *in basic
unmutated forms only* (Note 7: "where mutations have applied then of course a rather different
range of consonants is permitted"). /tʃ dʒ/ occur alone but **form no clusters at all** (Note 6);
/z/ is loan-only.

**Word-initial glide clusters (South, p. 100):** `C + G` is limited to **/hw/, /kw/, /gw/** and
(marginally) **/dj/**, the last often replaced by /dʒ/. `CCG` is exactly **/gwl/, /gwr/, /gwn/**.
**The South has /hw/ where the North has /χw/.**

**Medial and word-final singletons:** the **full** consonant set, in both positions (p. 81) — except
/h/, which is word-initial or immediately before a stressed vowel after a vowel or nasal, and
"in other medial positions and word-finally /h/ is ruled out" (p. 85).

**Glide clusters by position (Table 13):** G+G initial No / medial Yes / final No; G+C initial No /
medial Yes / final Yes; C+G initial Yes / medial Yes / final No.

**Sonority (p. 101):** `# O + S + G + Vowel + G + S + O #`.

**Repair of an illegal final cluster (Table 8, pp. 87–89):**

- **O + S — obligatory repair.** Monosyllable → **copy epenthesis** (the epenthetic vowel is
  *identical to the vowel of the monosyllable*; after a diphthong it copies the **glide**: /i/ after
  /j/, /u/ after /w/). Longer form → **delete one consonant of the cluster** (either one).
- **O + O and S + S — sometimes** repaired, by the same two devices.
- **S + O — never** repaired.
- An illegal **initial** cluster is never repaired: "it simply does not appear."

### (b) Vowel length, S vs N

**South (Fig. 1, p. 71).** Stress first; then /s ɬ/ as a special case keyed to final vs nonfinal;
then A/B/C on the following segment:

```
unstressed                        → SHORT
stressed, following /s/ or /ɬ/    → LONG   in a word-final syllable (= monosyllable or stressed final)
                                  → SHORT  in a nonfinal syllable   (= penult)
stressed, following A = /b d g v ð f θ χ/, zero   → LONG
stressed, following B = /m n ŋ l r/               → LONG OR SHORT  (lexical)
stressed, following C = /p t k/, any cluster      → SHORT
```

Southern diphthongs: **first element always short** (p. 97), the glides behaving as class C.

**North (Fig. 2, p. 75).** Same skeleton, but:
- **stressed nonfinal (penult) → SHORT unconditionally** — the whole A/B/C system applies only in
  word-final syllables;
- **/s/ and fricative+stop clusters move into A (LONG)**; **/ɬ/ and other clusters are C (SHORT)**.

**Mid Wales (Fig. 3, p. 77).** Stressed nonfinal → long and short **in free variation**; stressed
final → the southern or northern subroutine depending on where in the buffer you are.

**The two decisive differences for this project:**

1. **Awbery's South has the full length system in stressed PENULTS**, not only in monosyllables and
   stressed finals — p. 72 verbatim: "in the south all stressed syllables — monosyllables,
   penultimates and finals — behave alike." The single deviation is /s ɬ/, short in penults.
   `digest.md` §4.3 already reported the contrast (from `iosad2017`) but flagged a CONFLICT with
   `liu2018`; **Awbery resolves it in favour of the contrast**, on the right variety, with a full
   environment table. `digest.md` §9 open item 5 closes.
2. **/m/ and /ŋ/ are class B (LONG OR SHORT), not class C.** `digest.md` §4.3 row 4 puts them with
   the fortis stops in the SHORT class. Awbery's own prose (p. 67) is that long vowels before /m/
   are very few and before /ŋ/ absent, but he explicitly declines to call this systematic and
   leaves them in B. This is a CONFLICT, not a correction — see `digest.md` §4.

### (d) Loanword accommodation

**Awbery gives essentially none, and says so.** The chapter's only two statements are:

- **Note 2 (p. 103):** recent monosyllabic English loans are **excluded from the analysis** because
  they "are not integrated into the native vowel length system, and obscure its clear patterning."
- **Conclusion (p. 102):** "**In the past loans appear to have been integrated into the native
  system, but this no longer seems to happen. They are used unchanged even when their form conflicts
  with the normal requirements of Welsh**, for instance over the vowel length appropriate in a
  particular context."

Consequences for this project:

1. Awbery is **not** a source for §3 or §8. He supplies no adaptation rules. `parry1923`,
   `williams1994`, `buczek2014` and `wiki-cy-phon` remain the only §3 evidence.
2. His repairs (copy epenthesis, liquid deletion) are stated over **native morphology**
   (monosyllable ↔ inflected form), not over borrowing. They match the *pobl* / *ffenestr* triad
   `digest.md` §3.2 takes from `wiki-cy-phon`, and now have a primary source with the conditioning
   spelled out — **monosyllable → epenthesis, longer form → deletion** — which is exactly the split
   `welsh.rules [repair]` already implements.
3. His statement **cuts against** any "nativise the loan" reading of the modern layer, and so is
   evidence *for* spec §9.13's Welsh-first (L1) ordering being a **project choice** rather than an
   attested modern behaviour. It does not settle §4.4; it says modern Welsh does neither
   consistently.
