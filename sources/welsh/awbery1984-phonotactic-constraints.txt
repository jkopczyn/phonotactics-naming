A comparatively small number of factors have emerged repeatedly as relevant in determining these restrictions — the position of the item in the word, stress, and the immediate segmental environment. As these combine in various ways so the complexity of the resulting patterns increases. As so often in language an intricate pattern is created from very simple materials. 
 A number of questions however remain unanswered. At a very basic level there are quite serious gaps in the available information. Consider able fieldwork is needed to establish reliably where the boundaries tentatively sketched in on the maps in this contribution do in fact run. Is the apparent clustering of boundaries shown on Map | a mere artifact of the inadequate data base, or is it genuine? What is the nature of the transitions? In drawing a clear line on a map we imply a sharp switch from one pattern to another, but it is equally possible that the changeover is gradual, with one pattern slowly giving way to another in an area of some confusion. This is what we find with other phonological boundaries such as when we are dealing with the addition or loss of a phoneme. Only fieldwork can establish if it is true also in the case of phonotactic constraints. 
 It is also unclear if the assumptions on which this account is based are the most satisfactory possible. For instance we have phrased the whole account in terms of the word as a unit. What would be the effect of adopting a different unit of analysis, such as the syllable, morpheme or phrase? We have assumed that the opposition between long and short vowels is basic. To a considerable extent however, particularly in south Wales, consonant length covaries with vowel length. A short consonant follows a long vowel, as in /'karni/ ‘to sing’, and a long consonant follows a short vowel, as in /'kareg/ ‘stone’. What would the effect of turning the analysis on its head, and taking consonant length as basic? More drastically, what would be the effect of reconsidering the data presented here in terms of archiphonemes, prosodic analysis or genera tive phonology? 
 A third angle which has not been explored here is the question of change. How has the current pattern evolved in the past? Which dialectal variants are innovatory and which reflect earlier stages of the language? In what ways is the language evolving now? Probably the most important single influence on it at present is the widespread borrowing from English. In the past loans appear to have been integrated into the native system, but this no longer seems to happen. They are used unchanged even when their form conflicts with the normal requirements of Welsh, for instance over the vowel length appropriate in a particular context. What effect will this have on the

 NOTES 
 Data relating to Pembrokeshire derives from my own fieldwork, and I am indebted to Glyn E. Jones and Beth Thomas for information about the Llanwrtyd area and the Clwyd valley respectively. For convenience a phonemic framework is adopted here, with restrictions framed in terms of which phonemes may appear in particular positions. This should not however be taken as implying that further insights would not be derived from other approaches, and indeed some of the data discussed here is reanalysed in generative terms in Awbery (in press). 
 In this discussion we omit consideration of recent monosyllabic loans from English as they are not integrated into the native vowel length system, and obscure its clear patterning. A fuller treatment would of course need to examine the implications of this large-scale borrowing for the native patterns themselves. 
 There are some exceptional forms where a voiceless fricative is preceded by a short vowel, as with /os/ ‘if’, /dros/ ‘over’ and /b10/ ‘ever’ but such items form a very small minority of cases. 
 The boundaries drawn on all the maps in this chapter are perforce hesitant since the studies on which they are based are unevenly distributed. Further work is necessary to establish exactly where the boundaries run. 
 In north Pembrokeshire there are traces of the normal length patterning with the central vowel. In environments which normally require a long vowel it is sometimes found long, as in /'ka:van/ ‘whole’. Where the environment normally allows both long and short vowels or normally requires a short vowel then it is consistently short, as in /'kanar/ ‘early’ or /'askavn/ ‘light’.

 FEATURE-SPECIFICATION OF THE HIGH VOWELS 
 My concern will be with the high vowels, and with the relationship of the vowels [i] and [u] with the central vowel [9]. There are two questions to which I will address myself: 
 1. whether the distinctions carried by the surface segments [1], [4], [u] adequately reflect the structural contrasts within the phon ology, and 
 2. whether the central vowel [a] exists at all as an independent unit of structure within the phonology of the dialect. 
 My aim, in this first section of the paper, is to show how the internal structure of this fragment of the phonology of one dialect can be clarified once we cease to concentrate on the phonetic units which are the surface structure of the phonology and, instead, build an analysis on the structural relationships which underlie them.

 [i] [+] [u] +high +high +high —back | +back | +back —round —round +round 
 This specification squares with the traditional phonetic descriptions of these vowels in that [4] is conventionally viewed as sharing with front [i] the feature [—round], and with [u] the feature [+back] — hence the traditional label ‘high mixed’ for it. 
 The linking of [#] and [u] (through the shared feature [+back]) seems to be further justified when we examine an extended set of forms, in which [4] and [u] before a consonant occur in the same alternation, in which each is lowered to [a] when a monosyllabic word is extended by suffixation, and the vowel of the monosyllabic base becomes that of a penultimate syllable:
