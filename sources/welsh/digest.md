# Source digest: Welsh (Southern)

Target language for strand 2. Sources listed in `bib.md` in this directory. Citation keys are
bib.md keys; Wikipedia is cited as `[wiki-cy-phon §Section]`. Anything uncited or marked
`(unattested)` is the digest author's supposition, not a source claim. `CONFLICT:` lines keep both
sides.

## 0. Variety and scope

**Variety.** Southern Welsh (*De*), the southern member of the two-way N/S split every source uses
as its primary division [mayr-davies2011 p.2; breit2019 p.59]. In the traditional four-dialect
scheme, Dyfedeg (south-west) and Gwenhwyseg (Gwent/Morgannwg, south-east) are the southern members;
Powyseg is mid-Wales [wiki-cy-lang §The four traditional dialects]. Sources also separate, *within*
"South", a standard-southern system, a distinct south-western system, and a provisional
"non-enhanced" system [iosad2017 pp.30–31]; and a south-eastern area with its own consonant losses
[wiki-cy-phon §Fricatives].

**PHOIBLE row.** InvID 2406, source `ea`, "Welsh (Llanwrtyd)", 48 segments (29 C + 19 V), none
marked Marginal.

**The row's dialect label — RESOLVED in revision 2 by `awbery1984`.** Llanwrtyd is **Southern**,
and this is now a source statement rather than an inference:

- `awbery1984` **uses Llanwrtyd as one of its own data points.** Its note 1 (p.103) reads: "Data
  relating to Pembrokeshire derives from my own fieldwork, and I am indebted to **Glyn E. Jones and
  Beth Thomas for information about the Llanwrtyd area** and the Clwyd valley respectively"
  [awbery1984 p.103 n.1].
- Llanwrtyd is in the **historic county of Breconshire** (Brycheiniog), and Awbery draws the
  north/south line by historic county: "**North Cardiganshire and north Breconshire align with the
  south**, while the Dyfi basin and Llanfair Caereinion in Montgomeryshire are clearly northern"
  [awbery1984 p.72] — the boundary of Map 1 (vowel length in monosyllables).
- For consonant clusters the line sits further north still: "it is considerably to the north of the
  corresponding boundary shown on Map 1… **This time mid Wales is firmly in the southern camp**"
  [awbery1984 p.90, Map 6].
- Llanwrtyd falls outside all three of the chapter's special sub-areas: the south Cardiganshire /
  north Pembrokeshire "extended system" and the west Pembrokeshire /ə/ loss (Map 3, p.80), the
  north-west and south-east /e/→/a/ merger in unstressed finals (Map 4, p.82), and the south-east
  medial-stop devoicing (Map 5, p.84). It therefore takes the **normal Southern** value on each.

See `awbery1984-digest.md` §"What this settles" (c) for the map-by-map table. Two cautions carry
forward: the Map 2 (penultimate length) assignment rests on reading the drawn isogloss rather than
on a sentence, and Awbery's own note 4 (p.103) says every boundary in the chapter is "perforce
hesitant… Further work is necessary to establish exactly where the boundaries run."

**Why the earlier caveat was right to refuse the county inference.** The pre-Awbery reasoning below
is retained because it is still the correct treatment of the *modern* county name; what changed is
that a source now names the *historic* county, which is a dialect-relevant unit:

- **Y Bowyseg**, the *traditional dialect*. `breit2019` groups it with the **North**: "Y Wyndodeg …
  and Y Bowyseg 'Powys-ish' **in the north**, and Y Ddyfedeg and Y Wenhwyseg in the south", adding
  that it "is also often given the broad label of **Mid Welsh**" [breit2019 pp.53–54];
  [wiki-cy-lang §The four traditional dialects] likewise places Powyseg as north/central-eastern.
- **Powys**, the *modern administrative county* Llanwrtyd sits in. This is a 1974 local-government
  unit, not a dialect area.

Inferring the dialect from the **modern administrative county** would be an administrative-area ⇒
dialect inference with nothing behind it, and this digest still does not make it. `breit2019`
mentions neither Llanwrtyd nor the PHOIBLE survey, and central-western accents "tend to be treated
as hybrid forms" [mayr-davies2011 p.3].

**What resolves it is `awbery1984`, above** — not the county name, but a phonotactician placing
*north Breconshire* on the southern side of his own isoglosses and taking Llanwrtyd data from
Glyn E. Jones. The Y Bowyseg question is now moot for this digest: whatever traditional-dialect
label Llanwrtyd carries, Awbery's Southern rules are the ones stated to apply there.

The **segment list** in the row is independently and unambiguously Southern (no /ɨ/, eight
diphthongs), so §1 always stood on the row's own evidence. The N/S *tagging* of §§2–4 and §8 now
rests on `awbery1984` rather than on an assumption.

**Sources by weight for the South.** `williams1995` (the only explicit enumerated South phoneme
list, 32 C + 19 V = 51, from one male native South Welsh speaker) · `liu2018` (the one modern
instrumental study of Southern stress) · `mayr-davies2011` (10 South Wales speakers measured) ·
`iosad2017` (explicit N/S contrasts in length and quality) · `breit2019` (explicit Southern
statements on vowels, marginality, dorsal place) · `williams1994` (a **south-central Wales**
letter-to-sound system; underrated in `bib.md` — see §2.3) · `wiki-cy-phon` (pan-dialectal with N/S
notes). `jipa-north`, `mayr2009`, `lapsyd-cy` are **Northern**: every fact from them is tagged
**(North)** and used only as contrast.

### What the sources contradict in the PHOIBLE row

1. **The stop system is the row's biggest trap.** The row lists `p t k` **and** `pʰ tʰ kʰ` as
   separate phonemes and lists **no `b d ɡ` at all**. No source describes Welsh as having six oral
   stops. Every source describes a **two-way contrast in three places**, primarily aspiration:
   /pʰ tʰ kʰ/ fortis vs /b d ɡ/ lenis [breit2019 p.60]; "a fortis series and a lenis series"
   [jipa-north p.491] **(North)**; "/p t k/ are distinguished from /b d ɡ/ by means of aspiration
   more consistently than by voicing, as /b d ɡ/ are actually devoiced in some contexts"
   [wiki-cy-phon §Stops]; 31 speakers N and S: "both voicing and aspiration contribute … it is
   aspiration that is of major importance … the fortis-lenis distinction is more appropriate to
   describe the phonological system of Welsh than the voiced-voiceless one" [asmus2020 p.14,
   pp.11–13]. `williams1995`, the Southern source, writes the same contrast as p/b, t/d, k/g
   [williams1995 p.1 Table 1].
   **The row is coherent only read as `pʰ tʰ kʰ` = fortis, `p t k` = lenis** — the JIPA convention,
   with the lenis series written unaspirated rather than voiced. **Consequence for the tool: Irish
   /b d ɡ/ map onto the row's `p t k`, not onto nothing.**
2. **Nothing is marked Marginal**, though at least /z tʃ dʒ m̥ n̥ ŋ̥ r̥/ are marginal, loan-only or
   mutation-only in every source (§1).
3. **/ʍ/ is missing and is specifically Southern**: "Initial /χw/ is colloquially realised as [ʍ] in
   the south, e.g. *chwech* /χweːχ/ pronounced [ʍeːχ]" [wiki-cy-phon §Consonants] **(South)**.
4. **The labialised series /lʷ nʷ rʷ/ is missing** from the row but is in the canonical South list
   [williams1995 p.1] — contested, see §1.
5. **`χ` may be a Northern value in a Southern row** — CONFLICT 1 below.

### What the row gets right

- **No /ɨ ɨː/.** "The vowels [ɨː] and [ɨ] are found only in northern Wales" [iosad2017 p.6]; "This
  vowel has merged with high front /i/ in South Welsh" [breit2019 p.59]; Northern *tŷ* /tɨː/ and
  *ti* /tiː/ are homophonous [tiː] in the South [mayr-davies2011 p.3], with Southern *hîd* and *hûd*
  measured as "almost identical in the F1∼F2 space" [mayr-davies2011 pp.6–7 Table 3]. North has
  eight vowels the South lacks: /ɨ ɨː ɨʊ aɨ aːɨ ɔɨ ʊɨ əɨ/ [williams1995 p.4].
- **11 monophthongs in five length pairs plus /ə/** — the exact Southern count [mayr-davies2011 p.2
  Table 1; breit2019 p.59; iosad2017 p.6 Table 1; williams1995 p.1 Table 2].
- **Exactly 8 diphthongs**, and the right 8: "In South Welsh we have the full set of central and
  back vowels closing in /ɪ/ … as well as the full set of central and front vowels closing in /ʊ/"
  [breit2019 p.60]; Southern speakers "only distinguished eight categories spectrally"
  [mayr-davies2011 p.9]; Northern /ʊɨ ɨu əɨ ɔɨ aːɨ/ merge into /ʊi ɪu əi ɔi ai/ in the South
  [wiki-cy-phon §Diphthongs].
- **No /ʒ/.** `williams1995` names /ʒ/ (with /ɔː ɜː/) as one of "three extra segments that were
  purely English", added to a TTS system only to handle English input [williams1995 p.1].

### Remove

None outright: every segment in the row is attested for the South by at least one source. Two
sub-dialectal conditional removals:

- **/h/** disappears in all positions in parts of **south-east** Wales, and in those same dialects
  **/r̥/ is replaced by /r/** [wiki-cy-phon §Fricatives]. Also "never misused except in Gwent and

- **/m̥ n̥ ŋ̥/** are "missing entirely in some of the South Welsh dialects (these dialects have the

### Loan-only segments and their status

- **/z/** — loan-only, and **frequently nativised back to /s/** even in common loans
  [breit2019 p.62]. Historically absolute: "there is no z-sound in W, any such sound … is
  represented by s" [parry1923 p.238 §110], with the whole class going to /s/: *zeal* > *sel*.
  The modern orthography still nativises: *zero* > *sero* [wiki-cy-orth §Non-native letters]. But
  *jîns* [dʒɪnz] keeps it [wiki-cy-orth §Letter names].
- **/tʃ dʒ/** — loan-origin but **integrated in Southern Welsh** through native palatalisation of
  /t d/ before /i/ [breit2019 p.62]. Historically they were *not* available: Parry-Williams's
  English /tʃ/ and /dʒ/ both go to ⟨si⟩ = /ʃ/ with "apparently no trace of the dental stop"
  [parry1923 pp.225–227 §§87–88]. The modern layer keeps them: *jeli* [ˈdʒɛlɪ], *tsips*.
  **This is a period split the tool must choose between** (see §3).
- **/ʒ ɔː ɜː/** — not Welsh phonemes at all; added to `williams1995`'s system purely to cover
  English [williams1995 p.1]. Historically English /ʒ/ → Welsh /s/: *azure* > *asur*, *measure* >
  *mesur*, *pleasure* > *pleser* [parry1923 p.237 §106].
- **Loans sit outside the mutation system** in characteristic ways: *gêm* /ɡeːm/ does not undergo
  the /ɡ/-deleting soft mutation [wiki-cy-phon §Consonant mutations; bmj-mutations p.21]; *lamp,
  larum, letys, lwans* keep a voiced initial [l] instead of being fitted to the *ll-/l-*
  alternation [wood1988 p.231]. "The usual way of accommodating loanwords into Welsh" is to enter
  them as an **unmutated radical** [wood1988 p.231].
- **Loans carry unexpected length**: *gêm* [ɡeːm] is unexpectedly long, *ffres* [frɛs]
  unexpectedly short [breit2019 p.78 n.11].

### Notational quirks of the PHOIBLE row

1. `p t k` = **lenis**, `pʰ tʰ kʰ` = **fortis** (see §0). There is no voiced series in the row.
2. `d̠ʒ` / `t̠ʃ` are retracted-diacritic spellings of ordinary /dʒ tʃ/.
3. `ŋ̥` (ring below) vs the sources' `ŋ̊` (ring above) — same segment.
4. `ɑː` for the long low vowel follows Ball & Williams via [mayr-davies2011 p.2], but is called
   "misleadingly transcribed" by [wiki-cy-phon §Vowels] — see CONFLICT 2.
5. Diphthong offglides written `i`/`u`; [breit2019 p.60] writes `ɪ`/`ʊ`. Notational only.
6. `ɪu ɛu` where [williams1995 p.1 Table 2] writes `iʊ eʊ`. Same categories.
7. Length marks on `eː iː oː uː ɑː` only. `williams1995` marks length only on /aː/ and lets quality
   symbols carry the rest. Same 11 vowels either way.
8. `χ` — possibly a Northern value; CONFLICT 1.

### CONFLICTs in §0–§1

**CONFLICT 1 — is the Southern dorsal fricative /x/ or /χ/?**
- /x/ in the South, /χ/ in the North: "The voiceless dorsal fricative is usually /x/ in South Welsh
  and /χ/ in North Welsh" [breit2019 pp.61–62]; "described as uvular /χ/ in Northern Welsh, and as
  velar /x/ in Southern Welsh (Ball & Williams 2001)" [jipa-north p.494].
- Against: "The voiceless fricative /χ/ is realised as uvular except by some **southwestern**
  speakers, who produce a velar variant [x]" [wiki-cy-phon §Fricatives] — uvular is the general
  Southern value, velar confined to the south-west.
- `williams1995`'s explicitly Southern table prints **/χ/** [williams1995 p.1].
- Note breit2019 hedges within two pages ("usually /x/ in South" vs "Most dialects have uvular
  [χ]"). The PHOIBLE row has `χ`.
- **This is directly load-bearing for Irish /x/ → Welsh (see §8).**

**CONFLICT 2 — /ɑː/ or /aː/?** [mayr-davies2011 p.2] and the PHOIBLE row use /ɑː/;
[wiki-cy-phon §Vowels] calls that "misleadingly transcribed … The actual pronunciation of long /a/
is [aː], which makes the vowel pair unique in that for most speakers there is no significant quality
difference"; [breit2019 p.59] and [williams1995 p.1] write /aː/. **Mayr & Davies's own Southern
numbers support /aː/**: *hâd* 665/1253 vs *had* 667/1273 — "spectrally largely overlapping, yet
contrastive in duration" [mayr-davies2011 p.6]. Keep the row's `ɑː` as a label but treat the /a ~
ɑː/ pair as a **pure length contrast**, unlike the other four pairs.

**CONFLICT 3 — which consonants condition a long vowel in the South?**
- [williams1995 p.3] (South): long before /b d ɡ v ð f θ χ **m n ŋ** l r/; /s/ and /ɬ/ absent.
- [iosad2017 pp.7–8] (South, explicit): **short** before [pʰ tʰ kʰ **m ŋ**]; long before
  [b d ɡ f θ χ v ð]; long **or** short before [n l r] (lexical); **long before final [s ʃ ɬ]**.
- [wiki-cy-phon §Vowels] and [breit2019 p.77] side with Iosad, both listing **m, ŋ** as
  short-conditioning.
- **Three sources against one.** Treat `williams1995`'s /m n ŋ/ as an error or a TTS idiosyncrasy;
  and note it omits /s ʃ ɬ/, which is exactly the N/S-diagnostic environment.

**CONFLICT 4 — are /lʷ nʷ rʷ/ segments?** Yes [williams1995 p.1]; no, just lip-rounding on /ɡ l n r/
[wiki-cy-phon §Other consonants], and absent from [breit2019 p.61] and [lapsyd-cy].

**CONFLICT 5 — voiceless nasals: segments or /Nh/ sequences?** Segments [williams1995 p.1 Table 1]
(South); sequences [jipa-north p.493] **(North)**, [wiki-cy-phon §Consonants], [lapsyd-cy]. And
absent altogether in some Southern dialects [breit2019 p.60].

**CONFLICT 6 — is there one Southern vowel system?** [iosad2017 pp.30–31] distinguishes three
sub-systems (standard southern: tense/lax driven by length; south-western: "not all long vowels are
tense"; non-enhanced: duration only, no robust quality distinction) — but the standard-southern
conclusion rests on **a single speaker** [iosad2017 p.30 n.7]. [mayr-davies2011 p.3] treats all
Southern monophthongs bar /ə/ as tense–lax pairs differing in both quality and quantity.
## 2. Syllable structure and phonotactics

**Revision 2: `awbery1984` is now held in full and is the primary source for this section.**
See `awbery1984-digest.md`. It changes the standing of much of what follows, and the change is not
"here is the licit-cluster list we were missing" — Awbery never gives a list. He gives a **closed
set of constraints that generates one**, which is strictly better for rule-writing. §2.2A below
records the constraints; the per-cluster tables that follow are kept, re-tiered against them.

Hannahs 2013 and Czerniak 2015 remain closed. Everything still marked ASSEMBLED is the digest
author's inference and should be checked before it becomes a rule; but the tags below now read
against Awbery first and the class statements of `breit2019` / `jipa-north` second.

### 2.2A Awbery's constraint set — STATED, Southern, and complete as stated

**Internal constraints** — Awbery states these hold "throughout Wales without dialectal variation"
[awbery1984 p.86]:

1. A two-consonant cluster may be any of the four obstruent/sonorant types (`O+O`, `S+S`, `S+O`,
   `O+S`), and beyond constraints 2–3 "**there are no other restrictions on clusters of two
   consonants**" [awbery1984 p.86].
2. **A nasal and a following stop must be homorganic** — /ˈgʊmpas/, /ˈambeɬ/, /ˈkənta/, /ˈgʊndun/,
   /ˈwɪŋki/, /ˈdaŋgos/. "There are occasional exceptions… such as /ˈamkan/ 'guess' and /ˈamgilχ/
   'around', but they are rare." **No such requirement for a nasal + fricative** [awbery1984 p.86].
3. **Obstruent + obstruent clusters must agree in voicing** — "either both are voiced or both are
   voiceless": /ˈgʊðge/, /ˈɾagvir/, /ˈaskurn/, /ˈkɔpsi/ [awbery1984 p.86].
4. **Three-consonant clusters have exactly two shapes**: `/s/ + stop + liquid` (/ˈəsprid/ 'ghost',
   /ˈkaskli/ 'to collect') and `nasal + stop + liquid` (/ˈʃendler/ 'candlestick', /ˈmentro/ 'to
   dare') [awbery1984 p.86].

**Positional constraints** — Table 6 [awbery1984 p.87]:

| | Initial | Medial | Final |
|---|---|---|---|
| O + O | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| S + S | **No** | Yes | Yes |
| S + O | **No** | Yes | Yes |
| O + S | Yes | Yes | **No** |

Table 7's reading of the initial/final asymmetry: "in a cluster which consists of an obstruent and
a sonorant **the obstruent must be the outermost element, next to the word boundary**"
[awbery1984 p.87]. Extended to glides at p.101: `# O + S + G + Vowel + G + S + O #`.

**The O+S final ban is total in the South.** Table 9 [awbery1984 p.90]:

| | South | North |
|---|---|---|
| Fricative + Nasal | **No** | Yes |
| Fricative + Liquid | **No** | Yes |
| Stop + Nasal | **No** | No |
| Stop + Liquid | **No** | No |

with the single lexical exception /ˈbanadl/ 'broom' [awbery1984 p.90]. Southern /ˈoːvon/ against
Northern /ɔvn/ 'fear'; Southern /ˈɬəvir/ against Northern /ɬɪvr/ 'book' [awbery1984 p.89].

**Word-initial singletons** — Table 5 [awbery1984 p.83]: every consonant **except /χ/, /ð/, /ŋ/**.
Awbery notes these "cannot be expressed in terms of natural classes". Two riders, both important:

- **Note 7 (p.103) exempts mutation explicitly**: "we are referring here only to the **basic
  unmutated forms** of words. Where mutations have applied then of course **a rather different
  range of consonants is permitted**." This is direct source backing for the carve-out the plan's
  Known Deviation 9 (b) had to make on a coordinator ruling.
- **Note 6 (p.103)**: /tʃ/ and /dʒ/ "appear freely alone, but **do not form clusters with other
  consonants**"; /z/ "appears only in loans from English".

**Word-initial glide clusters, South** [awbery1984 p.100]: "in **most of south Wales only /h/, /k/
and /g/ may precede /w/, and only /d/ may precede /j/**" — /hweːχ/, /gwin/, /ˈkwaːrel/, /djawl/;
and "the /dj/ cluster is very marginal and is often replaced by the affricate /dʒ/". Three-consonant
initials with a glide are exactly **/gwl/, /gwr/, /gwn/** [awbery1984 p.99]. **The South has /hw/
where the North has /χw/** [awbery1984 p.100, Map 6].

**Medial and word-final singletons**: the **full** consonant set in both positions
[awbery1984 p.81], except /h/ (see §2.5).

**Glide clusters by position** — Table 13 [awbery1984 p.99]: `G+G` initial No / medial Yes / final
No; `G+C` initial No / medial Yes / final Yes; `C+G` initial Yes / medial Yes / final No.

**Repair of an illegal final cluster** — Table 8 [awbery1984 p.89]: `O+S` **obligatory**, `O+O`
sometimes, `S+S` sometimes, `S+O` **never**. The two strategies and their conditioning are in §3.2.
An illegal *initial* cluster is never repaired: "it simply does not appear" [awbery1984 p.87].

### 2.2 Onset clusters

**The one explicit statement in the whole open corpus** [breit2019 p.252], verbatim:

> "Welsh allows word-initial fricative-liquid ([fl, pr]), stop-liquid ([tl, pr]) and
> sibilant-liquid ([sl, sr]) sequences. It does not allow fricative-stop (*[ft, fp]) or
> fricative-stop-liquid sequences (*[ftl, fpr]). Yet, it allows sibilant-stop ([st, sp, sk]) and
> sibilant-stop-liquid ([stl, spr, skl])."

CONFLICT / probable typo: the same pair `[pr]` is printed for both the fricative-liquid and the
stop-liquid class. The string was verified against the PDF, so it is the author's, not an
extraction artefact. **As printed, this citation states only /fl/ for fricative+liquid.** /fr/ is
a typo-conjecture, listed below under TYPO-CONJECTURE and **not** as a stated cluster.

Onsets are given below in **four separate evidence levels**, because they are not interchangeable
for rule-writing:

- **A — STATED CLASS**: the source names the cluster as a member of a licit class.
- **B — LEXICALLY ATTESTED ONLY**: a source gives a word containing it; no class statement.
- **C — TYPO-CONJECTURE**: rests on emending a printed source.
- **D — DISPUTED ANALYSIS**: the sequence occurs but sources disagree whether it is an onset
  cluster at all (nucleus material, or a coda + empty nucleus).

**Re-tiering against `awbery1984` (revision 2).** Awbery's Table 6 licenses **every** `O+O` and
`O+S` cluster initially, subject only to the internal constraints of §2.2A. That promotes to
**STATED (Awbery)** every onset below that is obstruent-initial with an obstruent or sonorant
second member — i.e. the whole stop+liquid set `pl pr bl br tl tr dl dr kl kr ɡl ɡr`, the
fricative+liquid set `fl fr θr χl χr vl vr ðl ðr`, `sl sr`, the sibilant+stop set `sp st sk`, the
CCC set `spr stl skl str` (constraint 4: /s/+stop+liquid), and `ɡn kn`. The digest's old tiers B, C
and E for these are superseded; `/fr/` in particular no longer depends on emending `breit2019`, and
`/χr/ /χl/ /θr/` are no longer unattested inferences. **The tier letters in the tables below are
left as revision 1 wrote them, with an `→A(Awb)` marker added, so the revision-1 reasoning stays
auditable.**

- **`sm` and `sn` are not onsets** — they are `O+S` and so licensed by Table 6, but constraint 3

### 2.3 Coda clusters

**`awbery1984` supersedes the Northern template here.** Table 6 [p.87] licenses `O+O`, `S+S` and
`S+O` finally and bars `O+S`; Table 9 [p.90] shows the `O+S` ban is total in the South across all
four subtypes (fricative/stop × nasal/liquid), one lexical exception aside (/ˈbanadl/). The
internal constraints of §2.2A apply here too — so a final cluster must be homorganic if it is
nasal+stop, and must agree in voicing if it is obstruent+obstruent. **This is a STATED Southern
coda statement, and it replaces the ASSEMBLED sonority heuristic that closed revision 1's §2.3.**

Two consequences worth stating plainly:

- **`sb` is not a licit coda.** It violates constraint 3 (obstruent clusters agree in voicing).
  Welsh ⟨sb⟩ in *cosb* is the **spelling** of /sp/ — cf. §8.6's ⟨sb sg⟩ convention. Revision 1
  listed `sb` as a tier-B coda on the strength of the orthographic form. See CONFLICT-Awb-2.
- **Every remaining member of revision 1's coda list is `O+O`, `S+S` or `S+O`**, so Awbery licenses
  the list as a whole; the members below keep their word citations but their tier is now the class
  statement of a Southern source rather than a Northern one.

(/garð/ *gardd* 'garden'). Final obstruent + sonorant sequences (as in /disgɨpl/ *disgybl*) are

[williams1994 §2.1] describes a **south-central Wales** accent (near Llandovery) and states:

> /b, d, g, p, t, k, f, v, dh, s, x, th/ when followed by one of /l, n, r/, or between /m/ and /l/,
> at the end of a word."

So, word-finally in Southern Welsh, the rule is

    Ø → V / C₁ _ C₂ #     C₁ ∈ {b d g p t k f v ð s χ θ}, C₂ ∈ {l n r};  also C₁ = m, C₂ = l

and the corresponding clusters (/bl bn br dl dn dr gl gn gr pl pn pr tl tn tr kl kn kr fl fn fr vl
vn vr ðl ðn ðr sl sn sr χl χn χr θl θn θr ml/) are **disallowed word-finally** — though the same
sequences are fine as onsets. Examples: *aml* → /a m a l/, *llwybr* → /ɬ uɨ b i r/, *cefn* →
*cefen* [williams1994 §2.1, exx. 5–7].

revision 1 said "Southern word-final CC *tends* to falling or level sonority… treat as a heuristic,
not a closed phonotactic rule." `awbery1984` Table 6 + Table 9 make it a **closed rule**: every
`O+S` final is barred in the South, obligatorily repaired (Table 8), with /ˈbanadl/ the only stated
exception. `williams1994`'s C+{l,n,r} epenthesis class is one *instance* of Awbery's `O+S`
category, not an isolated list. The two sources agree and Awbery is the general statement.

Note also what Awbery adds that `williams1994` does not: **`O+O` and `S+S` finals are "sometimes"
repaired too** (Table 8) — /ˈguːðug/ 'neck' ~ /ˈgʊðge/ 'necks', /ˈheːlem/ 'cornstack' ~ /ˈhɛlmi/ —
so a licit-looking `O+O` final is not guaranteed to survive. Only `S+O` is "never" affected.
Rising-sonority finals in that class are repaired (see §3) and surface as clusters only when a vowel-initial
suffix re-syllabifies them into an onset: *pobl* [pɔbɔl] but *poblog* [pɔblɔg]
[breit-harris2014 p.1].

Historical corroboration: the epenthetic ⟨y⟩ "was not written where no audible glide developed, as
in *nt, rth, rs*" [morrisjones1913 §17(3) p.17] — i.e. falling-sonority finals never took
epenthesis.

### 2.4 Medial cluster limits

- "In Welsh, a single consonant between two vowels belongs normally to the second syllable; thus
  *ca|nu*, *gwe|le|dig*; when there are two or more consonants the first belongs to the first
  syllable, as *can|tor*, *tan|wydd*, *can|tref*" [morrisjones1913 §27 i p.30].
  → `C → onset / V _ V` ; `C → coda / V _ C V`. **Medial coda is maximally one consonant**
  [Hannahs via breit-harris2014 p.1].
- "The accented syllable is closed by the first of the two consonants… Even *i* and *w* cause the
  preceding consonant to close the penult; thus *glan|waith*" [morrisjones1913 §54 i(3) p.71].
- Medial CCC "appear to be consistently of the type s+stop+consonant" and are either heterosyllabic
  (*gwystlo* [gwɨs.tlo]) or compound-internal (*tystlythr* < *tyst*+*llythyr*; *trystfawr* <
  *trwst*+*mawr*) [breit-harris2014 p.1].
- Rising-sonority medial CC syllabifies as a complex onset: *cablu* [ˈkaː.blɨ], *goblygu*
  [goˈblə.gɨ] [Hannahs via breit-harris2014 p.1].
- Geminates are split across the syllable boundary [morrisjones1913 §27 i p.30], [jipa-north p.506].
- Degemination: `CC → C / [unstressed σ] _` — "a consonant which is etymologically double is
  simplified after an unaccented syllable" [morrisjones1913 §27 ii p.30].

| **/ɬ/ has no distributional restriction** (explicitly contrasted with /r̥/) | [breit2019 p.73] |
| **/h/ only word-initial or at the start of a stressed syllable**; "after a stress it generally disappears, e.g. *brenhínes* but *brénin*. Consequently /h/ is not found finally in any word" | [wiki-cy-phon §Fricatives]; [jipa-north p.503] "all but /h/ may appear word-finally" |
| **/h/ lost entirely in SE Wales**, and in the same dialects **/r̥/ is replaced by /r/** | [wiki-cy-phon §Fricatives]; cf. [morrisjones1913 §17 x p.19] "never misused except in Gwent and Glamorgan" |
| **Word-final /p t k/ occur only in modern loans** — "Modern Welsh does not therefore have etymological word final p t k. Where these occur today, they are in modern loans: *map* [mapː], *siop* [ʃopː], *mat* [matː], *cloc* [klokː]" | [wood1988 p.235] |
| Loans that kept a long vowel lenited the final C instead: *clog* [ˈkloːg] 'cloak' | [wood1988 p.235] |
| **/m̥ n̥ ŋ̥/ word-initial only via nasal mutation** of /pʰ tʰ kʰ/ | [jipa-north p.502] (N); [breit2019] |
| Voiceless nasals may split across a syllable boundary: *fy mhen* [ə ˈm̥hɛn]; *fy mhres* [ə ˈm̥r̥heːs] | [wiki-cy-phon §Other consonants]; [Hannahs via breit-harris2014 p.6] |
| **/w/ word-initial mainly in loans** (*waled*) or via soft mutation of /gw/ | [jipa-north p.504] (N) |
| Mutation-derived [w] counts as a **consonant** for the article (*y wasg*), [j] as a **vowel** (*yr iaith*) | [wiki-cy-phon §Diphthongs]; [morrisjones1913 §114 p.192] |
| **/v/ frequently deleted word-finally**: *tref* [tʰreːv] ~ [tʰreː] | [jipa-north p.503] (N) |
| **Welsh has no /z/** | [morrisjones1913 §17 ix p.19] |
| **/ʃ/** mostly in loans; in some **Southern** accents it is an allophone of /s/ after /ɪ iː/: *mis* [miːʃ] | [wiki-cy-phon §Fricatives] **(South)**; [morrisjones1913 §17 ix p.19] |
| **/χ/** is uvular [χ] generally, **velar [x] for some south-western speakers** | [wiki-cy-phon §Fricatives] |
| **/ə/ barred from final syllables and monosyllables** (it can only be short, violating word minimality); "It is also impossible in hiatus"; occurs only in closed syllables | [breit-harris2014 p.3]; [iosad2017 p.35]; [wiki-cy-phon §Vowel length] |
| **No antepenultimate stress**: "No Welsh word or word fully naturalized in Welsh is accented on the ante-penult" | [morrisjones1913 §43 i p.62] — this is what forces liquid *deletion* rather than epenthesis in *ffenestr* → [ˈfɛnɛst] |
| **Southern /l/ is light [l]** in onset and coda; /ɬ/ appears in /#_V/ and after /l/, [l] in /#Cl V/ and intervocalically | [jipa-north p.504]; [wood1988 pp.230–231] |
| Loans breaking the /#lV/ → [ɬ] pattern keep voiced [l]: *lamp, larum, letys, lwans* | [wood1988 p.231] |

### 2.6 Hiatus and glides

- **"w drops before w, and i drops before i"**, and "the syllable closed by the w or i remains
  closed after its loss": *cadwwn* → *cadwn*, *bedyddiir* → *bedyddir* [morrisjones1913 §36 i
  pp.39–40]. He notes the same explains the Welsh pronunciation of English *wood* as *'ood*.
- "i drops before u and w in monosyllables and final syllables" [morrisjones1913 §36 ii p.40];
  "w sometimes drops before o" (*go-, gor-* < *gwo-, gwor-*) [§36 iii p.40].
- V–V hiatus is licit and orthographically marked; a diaeresis goes between adjacent non-high
  vowels that cannot form a diphthong (*eo, ao, oa*) and between a diphthong and a following vowel
  (*chwaraewr* /χwaraɨur/) [williams1994 §2.1].
- Hiatus gives the Southern penult a **half-long** vowel: *lleol* 'local' [wiki-cy-phon §Vowel
  length] **(South)**.
- /ə/ is impossible in hiatus [iosad2017 p.35].
- Historic final /-Cw/ was consonantal and is now syllabic: *marw* [ˈmarʊ], *chwerw* [ˈχwerʊ],
  *hoyw* [ˈhɔjʊ] "were formerly monosyllabic, are pronounced in modern Welsh with two syllables and
  the consonant [w] is vocalised as the vowel [ʊ]" [wiki-cy-phon §Other consonants];
  [morrisjones1913 §42 p.61, §26 iv p.28]. But /w/ deletes between consonants: *meddwdod* →
  *medd-dod* [morrisjones1913 §42 p.61]; *marwdy*, *chwerwder* "the [w] is usually silent"
  [wiki-cy-phon].
- Orthographic disambiguation: *gẃraidd* /ˈɡʊ.raið/ (2 syll., hiatus) vs *gwraidd* /ˈɡwraið/
  (1 syll., onset cluster) [wiki-cy-orth §Diacritics].

### 2.7 Gemination

It exists, it is **post-tonic**, and it is at minimum a length effect; whether it is phonemic is
disputed.

    C → Cː / V́ _ V        (after a strongly stressed vowel, before an unstressed vowel)

- "This gemination only occurs after a strongly stressed vowel: thus the /t/ is long in *áteb*
  [ˈatːʰɛb] but short in *atébion* [aˈtʰɛbjɔn]" [wiki-cy-phon §Geminated consonants]. Measured
  **(North)**: *hapus* /pʰ/ 200 ms vs *hapusach* 124 ms; *ateb* /tʰ/ 209 ms vs *atebion* 134 ms
  [jipa-north pp.504–505 Table 2].
- Which consonants: "**p, t, c, m, s, ng, ll** are double after accented vowels, though written
  single; thus *ateb, canasant* = *at|teb, cána|s|sant*" [morrisjones1913 §27 i p.30]; "every medial
  or final *m, ng* or *ll* means *mm, ngng, llll* etymologically" [§54 i(2) p.71]. But "in the case
  of n and r the consonant is not necessarily double" — *cannu* vs *canu* [§54 note p.71].
- Modern erosion: Fynes-Clinton adds /f θ χ/; in modern Welsh /b d ɡ/ are also sometimes lengthened
  post-tonically; "the distinction between long and short /n, r, l/ mentioned by Morris-Jones
  appears to be generally no longer made, so that *tonnau* and *tonau* are usually pronounced
  identically, **except for the half-long vowel of the latter in southern dialects**"
  [wiki-cy-phon §Geminated consonants]. **(The South keeps the contrast on the vowel, not the
  consonant.)**
- Historical source: merger of two consonants [wiki-cy-phon]; worked out in [wood1988 pp.233–235].
  "Final m is always geminate" [wood1988 p.234].
- Wood's synchronic statement: `[+SYL] → [+LNG] / [+STR] _ C¹` — stressed vowels are long before at
  most one consonant; geminates sit in the underlying form (*pen* /pennː/) [wood1988 p.233].
- Provection (*calediad*) produces geminates: *agorodd* [aˈɡɔroð] vs *agor* [ˈakkɔr];
  /ˈɡlɨːb+hɛd/ → [ˈɡləppɛd] *gwlyped* [Hannahs via breit-harris2014 p.3].

CONFLICT: phonemic or allophonic? Hannahs represents post-tonic fortis consonants as geminates but
footnotes that they "might alternatively be ambisyllabic singletons"; Breit & Harris object that
this "is incompatible with his moraic account" [breit-harris2014 p.2 n.1]. [jipa-north p.504]
reports Hannahs as calling them ambisyllabic. [wood1988 p.233] treats them as underlying geminates.
Breit & Harris suggest the whole thing reduces to fortis/lenis: "it is surely not an accident that
the plosives Hannahs describes as moraic are fortis while their non-moraic counterparts are lenis"
[breit-harris2014 pp.2–3].

CONFLICT: which segments are "fortis" for coda-duration purposes. [grawunder2015 §3.3] splits
/-k -χ -f -ɬ -m -p -s -t -θ/ from /-b -d -ð -v -g -l -n -r/ by *relative* duration, but notes "the
absolute coda duration values would still group /-m/ with the 'lenis' obstruents".
[wiki-cy-phon §Consonants] gives the fortis set as /f θ χ p t k ɬ s/, without /m/.
[breit-harris2014 p.2] has /p t k m ŋ/ moraic.
## 3. Repair strategies (loanword adaptation)

**Period warning, and it is the most important caveat in this digest.** The bulk of the attested
data — `parry1923`, 653 of the 751 rows in `attested.tsv` — describes adaptation from **Middle and
Early Modern English**, mostly between roughly 1300 and 1700. Several of its rules are dead: /tʃ dʒ/
are now borrowed as /tʃ dʒ/, not as /ʃ/; final /p t k/ are now kept, not voiced. Rows carry the
donor period in the `note` column wherever Parry-Williams gives one, and carry `layer=historical`
in the new ninth column (§7). **Do not present a medieval adaptation as a modern rule.** Where the historical and the modern layer disagree, both are given
below, marked *(ME/early NE)* and *(modern)*.

### 3.1 Illicit onset cluster

#### #sC — the prothetic *y-*, stated precisely

This is the one place in the digest where three source statements, taken loosely, contradict each
other: §2.2 lists /st sp sk sl sr/ as **licit onsets**, while §3 and §8 apply a **prothesis repair**
to the same clusters — and the two source statements of the rule differ in **scope**. The
contradiction dissolves once scope, medium and conditioning are separated. All three are stated by
the sources; **none of them is chosen here.**

**(i) Scope — the two sources state the rule over different sets, and the difference is real.**

| Source | Scope as printed | Verbatim |
|---|---|---|
| [morrisjones1913 §23 ii p.26] | **s + ANY consonant** | "Initial s followed by a consonant has developed a prosthetic y-" — with native *ysgol*, *ystrad* |
| [parry1923 p.223 §84] | **s + STOP only** | "As in native words and Latin loan-words, W developed an on-glide **before s + stop** initially. … The E loan-words fell in with the general practice" |

So the broad rule (s + {p t k m n l r …}) is Morris Jones's; the loan-adaptation rule Parry-Williams
documents covers **only s + stop**. Parry-Williams's own examples are all s+stop: *scarlet* >
*ysgarlat*, *steward* > *ystiwart*, *standard* > *ystondardd*, *instrument* > *ysturmant*.
**The formal rule below is therefore given at s+stop scope, with the wider scope flagged.**

    (historical, ME/early NE, orthographic)   ∅ → y / # _ s{p, t, k}
    (Morris Jones's wider native scope)       ∅ → y / # _ s C

**(ii) Medium — spelling and pronunciation come apart, and both sources say so in the same terms.**

- [parry1923 p.223 §84]: "In colloquial speech, however, this prosthetic *y* is **hardly ever
  pronounced unless the accent falls on it**."
- [morrisjones1913 §23 ii p.26]: "In the spoken language it is **not heard except in words in which
  it is accented**, as *ysgol, ystrad, ysbryd* … but *sgubor, strodur, sgrifen, stryd*." In Old
  Welsh it was **not written at all** (*scipaur, strotur, scribenn*) and did not count as a syllable
  in the oldest verse.

**(iii) Conditioning — the vowel surfaces when it is stressed.** Both sources state the condition in
those words. Neither states the derivation of *when* it will be stressed; the observation that
Welsh's penultimate rule would place stress on the epenthetic vowel of a resulting trisyllable is
the digest author's derivation and is marked `(unattested inference)` in §8.6.

**Putting (i)–(iii) together, the three self-consistent encodings are:**

| Encoding | Rule | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **P — pronunciation-only** | #sC is a licit onset; never prothesise; spell ⟨sC⟩ | Matches all modern loans (*slogan*, *stiwdio*, *sgwlmaster* [wiki-cy-orth §Foreign words; buczek2014 p.345]) and Parry-Williams's own un-prothesised set (*sglont*, *sglefr*, *sglwtsh*, *sciabas* [p.225 §86], *stont*, *stondart* [p.242 §116], *spogen* [p.241 §114], *slwt* [p.242 §115]). Loses the *ysgol* look entirely |
| **S — spelling-only** | #sC is a licit onset; spell ⟨ys-⟩; do not add a syllable or move stress | Reproduces the orthographic tradition without claiming a phonological repair. Creates a spelling⇄IPA mismatch the romanizer must handle deliberately |
| **C — conditioned epenthesis** | Prothesise, and *pronounce* the vowel, only where it would carry stress | The only encoding faithful to both sources' stated condition, but it needs the stress derivation, which is unattested |

Whichever is chosen, **§2.2 and §3.1 are not in conflict**: /sp st sk sl sr/ are licit onsets in
every source, and the *y-* is an orthographic/stress phenomenon layered on top, not a phonotactic
repair of an illicit cluster. [breit2019 pp.252–253] independently analyses the /s/ of sC as a coda
+ empty nucleus, which would make ⟨ys-⟩ a fossilised writing of exactly that structure — i.e.
encoding **S**. See §8.6 for the Irish-side consequences.

**#kn is licit — Welsh does not repair it.** "Examples of E kn- retained as **cn-** in W: *cnaf*
'knave', *cnap*, *cnoc/cnocio* 'knock', *cnot* 'knot', *cnwpa* 'knob'" [parry1923 pp.220–221 §80],
with the explicit remark "There is no trace in W borrowings of a *t* sound" — Welsh took the cluster
intact and kept it after English lost it.

**#kw is illicit; #χw is not.** E *qu-* has two outcomes: retained as *cw-* (*quarrel* > *cwarel*,
*quart* > *cwart*, *question* > *cwestiwn*), or repaired to *chw-* (*quails* > *chwails*, *quarrel* >
*chwarel*, *acquittance* > *chwitans*, *quit* > *chwitio*). Parry-Williams's reason is explicitly
phonotactic: **"initial cw- is foreign to W … whereas initial chw is common"** [parry1923
pp.221–222 §82]. Both forms coexist for the same word. Likewise *wh-* > *chw-*: *whip* > *chwip*,
*whim* > *chwim*, *whistle* > *chwislo*, *whirligig* > *chwrligwgon* [parry1923 pp.229–230 §90].

**Medial cluster simplification.** "Consonants are usually suppressed in consonant-groups; sometimes
the apparent 'loss' is due to assimilation" — *company* > *cwmni*, *consumption* > *consymsiwn*,
*customer* > *cwsmer*, *partridge* > *petris*, *turnpike* > *tyrpeg*, *temptation* > *temtasiwn*,
*submit* > *smit* [parry1923 pp.234–235 §101b]. He notes English often has the same reductions, so
part of this is donor-side.

### 3.2 Illicit coda cluster — the *pobl / ffenestr / ewythr* triad

This is the single most digest-ready modern repair passage in the corpus
[wiki-cy-phon §Other consonants]. "When a word traditionally ends with a stop or fricative + /l/ or
/r/ … there is a tendency to adapt it in spoken Welsh to remove the cluster."

Three repairs, in a **conditioned order**:

1. **Copy epenthesis** — insert a vowel repeating the existing vowel, giving a disyllable:

        ∅ → Vᵢ / Vᵢ C _ {l, r, n} #

   *pobl* 'people' > [ˈpɔbɔl]; *cancr* 'cancer' > [ˈkankar]; *brwydr* 'battle' > [ˈbruɨdɨr]
   [wiki-cy-phon §Other consonants]; *cwbl* > [kʊbʊl] [breit-harris2014 p.1].
2. **Liquid deletion** — "**if this strategy would result in an antepenultimate accent**, the /l/ or
   /r/ may simply be deleted":

        {l, r} → ∅ / C _ # 	(when epenthesis would push stress to the antepenult)

   *ffenestr* 'window' > [ˈfɛnɛst]; *posibl* > [ˈpɔsib] [wiki-cy-phon §Other consonants;
   breit-harris2014 p.1]. The conditioning is the ban on antepenultimate stress: "No Welsh word or
   word fully naturalized in Welsh is accented on the ante-penult" [morrisjones1913 §43 i p.62].
3. **Metathesis** — "occasionally, with /θr/":

        Cθr# → Crθ#

   *ewythr* 'uncle' > [ˈewɨrθ] [wiki-cy-phon §Other consonants].

The epenthetic vowel's quality: [williams1994 §2.1] (**south-central Wales**) — it copies the
preceding syllable's vowel, or, after a diphthong, the diphthong's second element. Its distribution
is stated as a rule and is the best statement of Southern final-cluster illicitness we have:

    ∅ → V / C₁ _ C₂ #    C₁ ∈ {b d g p t k f v ð s χ θ}, C₂ ∈ {l n r};  also C₁ = m, C₂ = l

*aml* → /a m a l/; *llwybr* → /ɬ uɨ b i r/; *cefn* → *cefen* [williams1994 §2.1 exx. 5–7].
The clusters survive when a vowel-initial suffix resyllabifies them into an onset: *pobl* [pɔbɔl]
but *poblog* [pɔblɔg] [breit-harris2014 p.1].
**(North)** the same repair is optional and alternates with a syllabic consonant: *beibl*
[ˈpeipl̩ ~ ˈpeipil], *aml* [ˈaml̩ ~ ˈamal], *dwfn* [ˈduvn̩ ~ ˈduvun] [jipa-north pp.504–505].

Historical corroboration: the epenthetic ⟨y⟩ "was not written where no audible glide developed, as
in *nt, rth, rs*" [morrisjones1913 §17(3) p.17] — falling-sonority finals never took it.
Parry-Williams's loan data shows the same epenthesis before liquids: *cavalry* > *cafaltri*,
*commodore* > *combador*, *foolery* > *ffwlbri*, *Henry* > *Hendri* [parry1923 p.234 §101a], and a
modern example: *calendar* > *calendr*, pronounced as three syllables by svarabhakti
[buczek2014 p.348 n.9].

### 3.3 Word-edge processes

**Final voicing of English tenues — the biggest single historical rule** *(ME/early NE)*.
Environment stated explicitly [parry1923 pp.239–241 §§113–114]: **(1)** after a long vowel at the end
of a monosyllable; **(2)** after a vowel at the end of a polysyllable.

    {p, t, k} → {b, d, g} / Vː _ #  (monosyllable)   and   / V _ # (polysyllable)

*ape* > *ab*, *pope* > *pab*, *pipe* > *pib*, *gossip* > *gossib*; *coat* > *cod*, *estate* >
*ystad*, *basket* > *basged*, *pocket* > *poced*, *velvet* > *felfed*, *jacket* > *siaced*, *scarlet*
> *ysgarlad*; *cloak* > *clog*, *duke* > *dug*, *cassock* > *casog*, *garlic* > *garlleg*, *miracle*
> *miragl*, *treacle* > *triagl*, *music* > *miwsig*. He insists it is "a truly phonetic change, and
not merely due to a conscious (or unconscious) attempt to make the words sound (and look) more like
the general run of words in W."

**Blocked after a short vowel in a monosyllable** [parry1923 pp.241–242 §115]: *cap*, *clap*,
*cnap*, *cnot*, *het* 'hat', *plwc*, *slwt*. A clean length-conditioned complementarity.

*(modern)* The rule is dead as a rule but its output survives as a distributional fact: "Modern
Welsh does not … have etymological word final p t k. Where these occur today, they are in modern
loans: *map* [mapː], *siop* [ʃopː], *mat* [matː], *cloc* [ˈklɔkː]" [wood1988 pp.234–235] — with the
final consonant **geminated**, which is why the vowel stays short. "For loanwords where an original
long vowel has been retained, the final consonant is usually lenited": *clog* [ˈkloːɡ] 'cloak'
[wood1988 p.235]. **These two — geminate the final C, or lenite it — are the two ways a loan can
satisfy Welsh's length rule, and the choice determines the vowel.**

**Final devoicing after a sonorant** *(ME/early NE)* [parry1923 pp.242–243 §116]:

    {d} → {t} / {l, n, r} _ #

*gild* > *gilt*, *gold* > *golt*, *mould* > *molt*; *diamond* > *deiamwnt*, *ground* > *grwnt*,
*England* > *Inglont*, *pound* > *punt*, *lavender* > *lafant*; *board* > *bort*, *Edward* >
*Edwart*, *mustard* > *mwstart*, *guard* > *gwart*, *standard* > *stondart*.
Colloquial footnote worth having: "There is a tendency in colloquial speech to omit the r in
pronunciation, e.g. in Carns. *cwstat*, *ffwlbat*, *Edwat*" — i.e. **/rC#/ reduces to /C#/ in
speech** [parry1923 p.243 §116].

**Final spirantisation** [parry1923 p.243 §118]: `rd → rð`, `rt → rθ` — *board* > *bwrdd*,
*cupboard* > *cwpwrdd*, *bastard* > *bastardd*, *spikenard* > *ysbignardd*, *standard* >
*ystondardd*. "The change of rd to rð and rt to rθ is quite natural to W."

**Final lateral repairs** [parry1923 p.244 §§119–120]: `ld, lt → ɬt` (*bolt* > *bollt*, *malt* >
*mallt*, *Oswald* > *Oswallt*, *fold* > *ffollt*), and `-l(l) → ɬ` (*griddle* > *gradell*, *riddle* >
*rhidyll*, *mackerel* > *macrell*, *tun* > *tunnell*). Then "t was sometimes lost after final *ll*":
*ffollt* → *ffoll*. **Note this makes /ɬ/ a live repair target for a foreign final /l/.**

**Final nasal alternation** [parry1923 pp.246–248 §§126–127]: `n → m` — *button* > *botwm*, *cotton*
> *cotwm*, *pattern* > *patrwm*, *reason* > *rheswm*, *saffron* > *saffrwm*; paralleled natively and
especially in the **South** (*morwyn* > *morwm*). `n → ŋ` — *coffin* > *coffing* (parts of **SW**),
*dozen* > *dwsing*, *florin* > *ffloring*, *raisin* > *rhesing*; native SW parallel *prin* > *pring*.
And the reverse `ŋ → n`: *dubbing* > *dwbin*, *pudding* > *pwdin*, *fairing* > *fferin*.

**Consonant addition finally** [parry1923 pp.249–250 §130]:

    ∅ → t / {s, n, ɬ, f} _ #    ffalst 'false', ffirst 'fierce' (Dem. Dial.), Simwnt 'Simon',
                                Ystyphant 'Stephen', heisyllt 'hetchell', silfft 'shelf',
                                teligrafft 'telegraph'
    ∅ → p / m _ #               chwimp 'whim', swmp 'sum'
    ∅ → n / r _ #               miswrn 'vizor', masarn 'mazer', siswrn 'scissors', trenshwrn 'trencher'
    ∅ → n / _ s #               trespans 'trespass', relins 'relish', carains 'carriage', cwsberins 'gooseberries'
    ∅ → l / sg _ #              damasgl 'damask' (cf. native tymestl, cwrwgl)

Native parallels exist for all of these (*oll* > *ollt*, *bwyall* > *bwyallt*, *enghraifft*), so this
is a Welsh-internal preference, not an accident of the donor.

**Consonant loss finally** [parry1923 p.251 §131]: *Lincoln* > *Lincol*, *constable* > *kwnstab*,
*mustard* > *mwstar*, *vinegar* > *fineg*; final-cluster reduction *friends* > *ffryns*, *blinds* >
*bleins*; /n/ dropped before /s/: *orange* > *oraits*, *Lawrence* > *Lowres*; and **"Final t is often
dropped in colloquial speech"**: *Baptist* > *Batis*, *Methodist* > *Methodis* (Cards., **S.
Wales**).
CONFLICT: §130 adds /t/ after final /s/; §131 drops it. Parry-Williams does not reconcile them.
Reading: §130 is the written/older layer, §131's t-dropping is explicitly colloquial.

**Initial provection (voiced → voiceless)** [parry1923 pp.218–220 §§77–78]:

    {b, d, g} → {p, t, k} / # _

*bluff* > *palff*, *blanket* > *planced*, *bottle* > *potel*, *bank* > *ponc*; *destiny* > *tesni*,
*drop* > *tropio*, *damp* > *tamp*; *gear* > *cer*, *goblin* > *coblyn*, *grand* > *crand*, *gutter*
> *cwter*, *garrison* > *carsiwn*.
**The conditioning is morphological, not phonetic**: the loan kept its voiced initial where soft
mutation was expected (*yn grand*), so *g-* was reanalysed as the mutation of *c-* and a new radical
*crand* was back-formed. Parry-Williams hedges: "This, of course, may not account for all these
cases." Most examples are late or dialectal.
**Initial voicing** goes the other way [parry1923 p.220 §79]: *posy* > *bwysi*, *prologue* >
*brolog*, *carrots* > *garetsh* (Dem. Dial., **S. Wales**), *croft* > *grofft*. He gives no
conditioning and hedges every item ("The following *may* be examples").
CONFLICT (apparent, resolvable): §78 and §79 point in opposite directions. They are not competing
rules — both are symptoms of the same mutation-driven instability in the radical form, which can run
either way. §78 has dozens of examples; §79 has a hedged handful.

**Initial l-, r- fortition** [parry1923 p.231 §95]:

    l → ɬ / # _    ;    r → r̥ / # _        "being now foreign to W as radical (unmutated) forms"

*loft* > *lloft*, *leopard* > *llewpard*, *loin* > *llwyn*; *remedy* > *rhymedi*, *ropes* > *rhobs*,
*raisin* > *rhesing*. **But many loans keep plain l-/r-**: *lawnt*, *lili*, *lamp*, *larder*,
*recorder*, *redi*, *rwbel* — "the later borrowings naturally tend to preserve the l and r
unchanged." Corroborated for the modern layer: *lamp, larum, letys, lwans* keep voiced [l]
[wood1988 p.231]. **This is a live choice, not a rule: initial /l/ can become /ɬ/ or stay /l/.**

**Initial prothetic g- before a vowel** [parry1923 p.221 §81]: *honest* > *gonest*, *order* >
*gordro*, *yield* > *ildio* → *gildio*. Same reanalysis motive (a vowel-initial form looks like the
soft mutation of a *g-* radical); cf. native *allt* → *gallt*.
**Initial w- > gw-** [parry1923 p.228 §89]: *welt* > *gwalt*, *warrant* > *gwarant*, *warden* >
*gwarden*, *Windsor* > *Gwinsor* — "Initial w (consonantal) is foreign to W as a radical (unmutated)
form." Later borrowings keep *w-*: *weir* 'wire', *wits* 'witch', *waets* 'watch'. And **/w/ deletes
before a rounded vowel**: *wood-knife* > *wdcneiff*, *worsted* > *wstyd*.

**Initial h-loss** [parry1923 p.230 §92]: *horrible* > *oribl*, *hostess* > *ostes*, *Hilary* >
*Ilar* — he attributes this to English h-dropping variation, not to a Welsh repair.

**m/b interchange** [parry1923 pp.230–231 §§93–94]: *Milan* > *balaen*, *mule* > *buwl*; *banner* >
*maner*, *bacon* > *macynau*, *bargain* > *margen*. Same mutation-reanalysis mechanism.

### 3.5 Vowel adaptation

**Length is not transferred as length.** Parry-Williams is emphatic that English long vowels land as
plain Welsh vowels; the *quality* correspondence instead encodes **which stage of the Great Vowel
Shift the donor was at**, and is therefore a dating diagnostic rather than a rule
[parry1923 pp.122–186 §§24–51]:
- long open ē → W *e*: *beaver* > *befer*, *please* > *plesio* [p.122 §24]
- long close ē → W *i*: *beer* > *bir*, *friend* > *ffrind* [p.125 §25]
- long ī, pre-shift → W *i*; long ī, post-shift (~1500) → W **ei**: *bible* > *beibl*, *bribe* >
  *breib*, *pint* > *peint* [p.149 §33] — explicitly **later (NE)** than the plain-*i* set
- long ō → W *o* ("Welshmen still very often pronounce such E words as *cloak, roast*, without any
  trace of diphthongization") [p.183 §48]; close ō → *o* or *w*: *fool* > *ffwl* ~ *ffol* [p.186 §51]
- E long *u* ("duke"-type) → W diphthong *iw/uw*: *music* > *miwsig*, *sugar* > *siwgr* [p.206 §66]

**Diphthongs.** ME *ai/ei* → W *ei*, or **ae** before /l m n r s/, before a vowel, or finally in
monosyllables (*heir* > *aer*, *tailor* > *taeliwr*), reducing to *e* when unstressed (*bargain* >
*bargen*) [pp.190–196 §§54–56]. *oi/oy* → W **wy** (*point* > *pwynt*, *anoint* > *anwyntio*)
[p.197 §58]. *au* before nasals → W **aw** (*dance* > *dawns*, *haunt* > *hawnt*) — Welsh preserves
the older Anglo-Norman nasalised *au* that English itself abandoned [p.201 §61]. ME long *u* →
*aw* or *ow* (*soldier* > *sawdwr*; *powder* > *powdr*, *court* > *cowrt*) [pp.208–209 §68].

**Environment-conditioned splits** he flags explicitly:
- **§§69–74, before sibilants** [pp.212–215]: a sibilant (or /n r/ + sibilant) triggers an *i*-glide
  in the preceding vowel. *a* → ae/ai (*cage* > *caets*, *orange* > *oraens*, *vantage* > *mantais*);
  *e* → ei (*vengeance* > *feinsians*, *trencher* > *treinsiwr*, *fresh* > *freiss* beside
  undiphthongised *ffres*); *o* → oe (*broach* > *broes*); *u* → wy (*bushel* > *bwysel*, *touch* >
  *twyts*).
- **§75, before /l/** [pp.216–217]: *a*+l → **aw** (*gall* > *gawl*); *o*+l → **ow** (*bowl* > *bowl*,
  *bold* > *powld*, Carns., **N. Wales**); *u*+l → **ow** (*poultice* > *powltis*, *bolt* >
  *powlto*, both **Demetian, S. Wales**).

**Unstressed vowels: Welsh does NOT substitute schwa.** He states plainly that Welsh takes a full,
"clear" vowel (*a*, *e*, *y*, *i*) for a reduced English vowel, the choice depending on which of the
two competing English reduction tendencies was operative in the donor dialect at the time
[parry1923 §§13–15, 17, 27]. Short *i* → obscure *y* before /l n r s/ (*anise* > *anys*, *ermine* >
*ermyn*) [p.128 §27a]; short *e* → *a* or *y* in unstressed syllables (*dagger* > *dagyr*,
*parliament* > *parlmant*) [p.90 §14]. **This matters for Irish input: unstressed Irish /ə/ should
not be assumed to survive as Welsh /ə/ in the ultima — /ə/ is barred from the final syllable
entirely** [breit2019 p.60; wiki-cy-phon §Vowels].
*(modern)* the same ban shows up as a repair: E *umbrella* → W *ymbarel*, final-stressed **because**
the English final schwa was simply elided [buczek2014 p.347 n.8].

### 3.6 Other reported processes

**Every bullet whose citation is `parry1923` is historical evidence, not a modern rule.** Each
carries its period inline below; do not lift one into a rule file without it.

- **Metathesis** *(ME/early NE)* [parry1923 p.234 §100b]: *barrel* > *balir*, *mallard* > *barlat*, *alderman* >
  *aldramon*, *instrument* > *ysturmant*, *ignorant* > *ingrant*.
- **Dissimilation of /r … r/ to /r … l/** *(ME/early NE)* [parry1923 p.249 §129]: *corner* > *cornel*, *dresser* >
  *dresal*, *razor* > *rasal*, *sulphur* > *syffal*.
- **Medial provection** *(ME/early NE)* [parry1923 pp.232–233 §97]: voiced stops devoice next to /w l r/, next to
  another consonant, and in geminates — *woodward* > *wtwart*, *tables* > *taplas*, *bedgown* >
  *betgwn*, *cog* > *cocio*, *trigger* > *tricar*.
- **Front glide after velars** *(late/NE, and dialectal — Caernarfonshire, i.e. NORTH Welsh, and
  obsolescent: Parry-Williams reports it "as spoken by the older people")* [parry1923 p.221 §81a]: `∅ → j / #{ɡ,k} _ V[+front]` — *guard* >
  *giard*, *gate* > *giat*, *game* > *giam* (N. Wales, Carns., borrowed from an English stage that
  had the glide) — **and then generalised inside Welsh**, so older Carns. speakers say *ciartra* for
  *cartref*, *ciath* for *cath*, even before non-front vowels. **This is the closest thing in the
  corpus to a Welsh mechanism for a palatal onglide; see §8.**
- **Haplology / syllable deletion** *(colloquial, N. Wales for the Parry examples)*
  [parry1923 pp.231–232 §95a]: *papurau* > *pur(a)u*, *society* >
  *seiat* (colloquial, N. Wales). Modern: syncope and apocope are used as an *alternative to stress
  shift* — 32 items where deleting a syllable makes the source-stressed vowel penultimate:
  *astronomy* > *astronmi*, *destiny* > *tesni*, *liquorice* > *licris*, *society* > *seiat*,
  *wheelbarrow* > *whilber* [buczek2014 p.345].
- **Gemination of the loan's final consonant** as the length-satisfying repair: *map* [mapː], *siop*
  [ʃopː], *mat* [matː], *cloc* [ˈklɔkː] [wood1988 pp.234–235].
- **Loans enter the mutation system as unmutated radicals** [wood1988 p.231], and some stay outside
  it: *garej, ga:t, ge:m, glasses, good, greedy* are exempt from the /ɡ/-deleting soft mutation
  [bmj-mutations p.21]; *lamp, larum, letys, lwans* keep initial voiced [l] [wood1988 p.231].
  **Personal names do not mutate at all** in the spoken corpus [bmj-mutations p.21] — directly
  relevant to a name generator.

### 3.7 Stress re-assignment in loans *(modern; from buczek2014)*

Welsh is **not** a case of mechanical penultimate assignment [buczek2014 p.335]. Five outcomes,
all attested:

1. **Full shift to the penult** — the largest class, 80+ underived and 120+ derived items
   [buczek2014 pp.345–346]: *gooseberry* > *gwsberi*, *post-office* > *postoffis*, *courtesy* >
   *cwrtesi*, *present* > *presennol*.
2. **Shift achieved by deleting a syllable** instead of moving stress (32 items) [p.345]: see §3.6.
3. **Final stress retained** (42 items) [p.347] — tolerated because final stress is independently
   native: *appeal* > *apêl*, *brigade* > *brigâd*, *cassette* > *cysêt*, *cocoon* > *cocŵn*.
   Note the circumflex both marks the length and, in effect, marks the stress.
4. **Antepenultimate stress imported** — "prosodically the weakest [position] in Welsh … a clear
   indication of the foreign provenience of an item" [p.348]: *elephant* > *eliffant*, *paragraph* >
   *paragraff*, *testament* > *testament*, *ambulance* > *ambiwlans*, *hooligan* > *hwligan*,
   *bungalow* > *byngalo*. Neo-classical compounds are a systematic sub-case (46+ forms) [p.349].
5. **Morphological integration forces regularisation** — the most robust generalisation in the
   paper: "all borrowed nouns (save two cases) when pluralised, shift stress to this default site,
   regardless of where it was placed in the process of borrowing … when a word enters the
   morphological component of Modern Welsh it becomes phonologically native and fully regularised"
   [buczek2014 p.347]. *eliffant* → *eliffantod*; *testament* → *testamentau*; *ambiwlans* →
   *ambiwlansys*; *pensiwn* → *pensiynau*.

Age of loan is the main predictor: "Older loans tend to be fully assimilated, while more recent
(also more specialised) loanwords normally retain their (nearly) English pronunciation"
[buczek2014 p.339]. And: "**in Welsh the long vowels are characteristically found only in accented
syllables, thus if length is preserved in the loan, it will naturally attract word-stress**"
[Thomas 1997:72 via buczek2014 p.343] — **this is the single most useful fact for Irish input, whose
long vowels are lexically fixed.**

`(unattested)` for any Southern-specific loan-stress statement: buczek2014 draws **no** N/S
distinction anywhere.

## 4. Stress and length

### 4.1 Stress rule

    stress → penultimate syllable        (polysyllables)
    stress → the only syllable           (monosyllabic content words; monosyllabic function words are unstressed)

[liu2018 p.6; williams1983 p.22; breit2019 pp.74–75, "regardless of length or diachronic origin"].
*tad* [ˈtaːd] → *tadau* [ˈtada]; *blynyddoedd* [bləˈnəðɔɪð] [liu2018 p.6].

**Stress shift under suffixation** — adding a syllable moves stress rightward
[wiki-cy-phon §Stress and pitch; breit2019 p.74]:
*ysgrif* /ˈəsɡrɪv/ → *ysgrifen* /əsˈɡrivɛn/ → *ysgrifennydd* /əsɡrɪˈvɛnɨð/ → *ysgrifenyddes*
/əsɡrɪvɛˈnəðɛs/ → *ysgrifenyddesau* /əsɡrɪvɛnəˈðɛsai/. Likewise *agor* [ˈaːɡɔr] → *agoriad*
[aˈɡɔrjad] → *agoriadau* [ˌaɡɔrˈjaːdaɨ]; *gair* → *geiriad* → *geiriadur* → *geiriaduron*
[breit2019 p.74].
**Note the length interaction in that series: a vowel is long only while it is stressed, and a
previously short unstressed vowel lengthens when stress lands on it** [breit2019 p.76]. For a
generator this means length must be assigned *after* stress, not carried from the input.

**Exceptions** [wiki-cy-phon §Stress and pitch; breit2019 pp.75–76]:
1. Native final stress from historical coalescence — verbs in *-áu*, *Cymraeg* [kəmˈraɨɡ]. Tied to
   specific affixes: the nominaliser *-had* (*parhad* [parˈhaːd]), the verbaliser *-hau* (*parhau*
   [parˈhaɨ]), and the reflexive prefix *ym-*.
2. Prefixes that do not reliably take stress: *di-* 'without' → *diwerth*, final-stressed.
3. Borrowings: "Antepenultimate stress is found exclusively in borrowings from English" —
   *testament* [ˈtɛstamɛnt], *polisi* [ˈpɔlɪsi], *paragraff* [ˈparaɡraf]; and final-stressed loans
   *parêd* [paˈreːd], *canŵ* [kaˈnuː], *sigâr* [siˈɡaːr], *balŵn* [baˈluːn] [breit2019 p.75].
4. Morphological pairs: *ymladd* /ˈəmlað/ 'to fight' vs *ymlâdd* /əmˈlaːð/; *ystôl* [əsˈtoːl]
   [liu2018 p.6].

**Compounds** are usually penultimate (*gwínllan*), but some are pronounced as two words with
secondary + primary stress: *cyn-faer* [ˌkɨnˈvaɨr] 'ex-mayor' [wiki-cy-phon §Stress and pitch].

**Secondary stress.** Placement: "appears in words with four syllables or more … alternating, falling
on the second syllable to the last stressed syllable" — *bendigedig* [ˌbɛndɪˈɡɛdɪɡ], *caniatâd*
[ˌkanɪaˈtaːd] [liu2018 pp.3, 6; breit2019 p.74].
**But liu2018 found no acoustic correlate of secondary stress in Southern Welsh at all** — not F0
(p=0.22, and in the *opposite* direction to primary stress), not duration (p=0.42), not RMS energy
(p=0.35), not F1 (p=0.51) or F2 (p=0.74) [liu2018 pp.11–17]. "It is possible that there is little to
no phonetic signal for secondary stress in Southern Welsh" [liu2018 p.25]. The one place secondary
stress has segmental consequences is compounds, where ⟨y⟩ takes its final-syllable value [ɨ] and long
vowels can be preserved: *Casnewydd* [ˌkaːsˈnewɨð] 'Newport' [wiki-cy-phon §Stress and pitch].

### 4.2 Acoustic correlates — the received picture and the Southern instrumental one

The **received picture** (Williams 1983, and everyone who cites her) is that Welsh stress runs
opposite to the cross-linguistic default. Welsh listeners located stress on syllables with **shorter
duration, lower envelope amplitude, and f0 change under 15 Hz**; two monolingual English listeners
chose the opposite parameters [williams1983 pp.30–31; coleman-stress §Week 1; liu2018 pp.3–4].
"Stress in Welsh is identified with shorter duration of the stressed vowel and longer duration of
its following consonant. The finding is all the more interesting for not being the obvious one"
[williams1983 p.82]. Consequence: English speakers consistently hear Welsh stress on the *final*
syllable, and are consistently wrong [Rees 1977 via williams1983 p.23; buczek2014 p.342].

**Post-stress consonant lengthening is the one correlate every source agrees on.**
*ymladd* /ˈəmlað/ has a longer /m/ than *ymlâdd* /əmˈlaːð/ [williams1983 p.53 via wiki-cy-phon];
*áteb* [ˈatːʰɛb] vs *atébion* [aˈtʰɛbjɔn] [wiki-cy-phon §Geminated consonants]; measured **(North)**
*hapus* /pʰ/ 200 ms vs *hapusach* 124 ms [jipa-north pp.504–505]. "Welsh listeners use the duration
of the PSC as the primary cue to judge vowels as stressed" [pmc-lexical-stress §Introduction]. It
carries over into Welsh English: Rhondda Valley speakers "instead of lengthening the stressed
vowel … markedly shorten it and lengthen the succeeding consonant" [Walters 2003 via
pmc-lexical-stress].

**High pitch on the following unstressed ultima** is the other received claim: "stress on
penultimate syllables is characterised by a low pitch, which is followed by a high pitch falling to
a low pitch on the (unstressed) word-final syllable" — thought to be a remnant of the Old Welsh
final accent, the stress having shifted without the pitch [wiki-cy-phon §Stress and pitch];
"the final syllable, and not the stressed penultimate, is pronounced with the most prominent pitch
movement and with much greater force" [buczek2014 p.340].

CONFLICT (the important one). **`liu2018`, the only instrumental study of Southern Welsh with a
proper unstressed control group, reverses both claims:**
- Primary-stressed vowels are **LONGER** than unstressed, by 38.62 ms (97.52 vs 58.90; p<0.001)
  [liu2018 pp.12–13]. Liu attributes Williams's result to an artefact: Williams compared stressed
  syllables to other syllables *in the same word*, i.e. mostly to word-edge syllables inflated by
  final lengthening [liu2018 pp.3–4, 23].
- Ultima vowels are longer still (125.74 ms), ~26 ms longer than stressed penults — so the *relative*
  impression Williams recorded stands even though the absolute claim does not [liu2018 p.23].
- Ultima F0 is **LOWER** than unstressed (p<0.003), with a monotonic fall from the stressed penult
  onset to the ultima offset and "no visible peaks or valleys"; "the ultima vowel does not seem to
  bear pitch prominence" [liu2018 pp.11, 24]. Liu flags a possible confound: ~60% of her ultima
  tokens preceded the carrier word *i* /ɪ/ and underwent glottal epenthesis, which lowers F0
  [liu2018 p.25].
- Intensity shows **no** significant effect of primary stress (p=0.12) [liu2018 pp.14–15].
- Vowel quality: no overall F1/F2 effect, but stressed /ɪ/ has higher F2 and stressed /ɔ ə/ lower —
  unstressed reduction is **horizontal (front–back centralisation), not vertical**
  [liu2018 pp.17–18, 28].

Not resolved. Williams is the standard reference; Liu is the better-controlled study on the right
variety. **The tool's romanization has to choose explicitly**, because the two give opposite
instructions for how a stressed penult should be read.

### 4.3 Vowel length — the Southern rule

Long vowels are restricted to **stressed** syllables in all dialects [iosad2017 p.7]. /ə/ has no long
counterpart [iosad2017 p.6] and is barred from the ultima [liu2018 pp.6–7]; it *can* bear stress,
unlike English schwa [liu2018 p.7].

**In the South the length contrast survives in both stressed penults and stressed final syllables.**
Minimal pair in the penult: *tonau* [ˈtʰoˑnɛ] 'tunes' vs *tonnau* [ˈtʰɔnɛ] 'waves'; long vowels in
penults are phonetically shorter than in final syllables — **"half-long"** [iosad2017 p.7].
**(North)** stressed penult vowels are **always short** and the pair is impossible, both [ˈtʰɔna]
[iosad2017 pp.7–8; wiki-cy-phon §Vowels: *cath* long, *cathod* short].

### 4.3A `awbery1984` Fig. 1 — the Southern decision tree, and how it compares

Awbery gives the rule as a decision tree over three factors, in order: **stress**, then **final vs
nonfinal syllable**, then **the immediately following segment** [awbery1984 p.70, p.71 Fig. 1].
"Final" means *monosyllable or stressed final syllable*; "nonfinal" means *penult* — his own gloss,
p.70.

```
unstressed                                        → SHORT
stressed, following /s/ or /ɬ/, word-final syll.  → LONG
stressed, following /s/ or /ɬ/, nonfinal syll.    → SHORT
stressed, following A = /b d g v ð f θ χ/, zero   → LONG
stressed, following B = /m n ŋ l r/               → LONG OR SHORT   (lexical)
stressed, following C = /p t k/, any cluster      → SHORT
```

Southern diphthongs: **the first element is always short**, the glides behaving as class C —
"glides appear to side rather unexpectedly with voiceless stops and consonant clusters which also
require that a preceding vowel be short at all times" [awbery1984 p.97].

**Row-by-row against the seven-row table below:**

| Digest row | Awbery | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 1. `V → Vː / ˈ_ #` (open final) | class A includes **zero** → LONG | **agrees** [p.71] |
| 2. `V → Vː / ˈ_ {b d ɡ v ð f θ χ} #` | class A exactly | **agrees** [p.71] |
| 3. `V → Vː / ˈ_ {s ʃ ɬ} #` (South only) | /s ɬ/ → LONG in a word-final syllable | **agrees** [p.71]; Awbery does not mention /ʃ/ in the tree, but lists it among the voiceless fricatives and gives /miːʃ/ 'month' long [p.81, p.83] |

**CONFLICT-Awb-3 — /m/ and /ŋ/.** The digest's row 4 puts /m ŋ/ with the fortis stops in the SHORT
class. Awbery puts them in class B (LONG OR SHORT). But his own prose undercuts his own table:
"**Very few long vowels appear before /m/; no long vowels at all appear before /ŋ/**" — the single
example is /biːm/ 'I was' — and he explicitly declines to decide whether this is systematic or
accidental, adding that "accidental gaps will be ignored in the body of this chapter"
[awbery1984 p.67]. So the *table* says lexical and the *prose* says short-in-practice.
**Unresolved.** For a generator the practical outcome is the same (short), which is what
`welsh.rules` emits; the conflict is recorded rather than acted on.

**CONFLICT-Awb-4 — the big one: Southern stressed PENULTS.** See §4.3B.

### 4.3B Southern penults: `awbery1984` resolves §9's open item 5

Revision 1 recorded a CONFLICT (below) over whether length is contrastive in Southern penults —
`iosad2017`, `wiki-cy-phon`, `williams1983` and `breit2019` say yes; `liu2018` says length is
contrastive only in monosyllables. **`awbery1984` comes down squarely on the "yes" side, for the
right variety, with the full environment table**, and is the most explicit statement available:

> "**In the south all stressed syllables — monosyllables, penultimates and finals — behave alike.
> Before a single sonorant vowel length is contrastive; elsewhere the choice of a long or short
> vowel is predictable from what immediately follows.** In the north however this parallelism is
> lost." [awbery1984 p.72]

Table 2 [p.69] gives the two columns side by side, and the **only** difference between a Southern
monosyllable and a Southern stressed penult is /s/ and /ɬ/ — long in the monosyllable, short in the
penult. Everything else — voiceless stop, cluster, nasal, liquid, voiced stop, voiced fricative,
/f θ χ/, zero — is identical. Worked pairs: /ˈaːraɬ/ 'other' vs /ˈkareg/ 'stone'; /ˈkaːder/ 'chair'
vs /ˈateb/ 'to answer' [p.74], all four with the North's short vowel for contrast.

Table 4 [p.74] states it as a grid:

| | South | North |
|---|---|---|
| Monosyllables | LONG, SHORT | LONG, SHORT |
| Finals | LONG, SHORT | LONG, SHORT |
| **Penultimates** | **LONG, SHORT** | **— SHORT** |

**This is a behavioural change for the tool**, not just a digest note: `welsh.rules [post-stress]`
was written to leave stressed penults short, citing exactly the CONFLICT that Awbery now settles.
See §9 item 5 and the rule file's `[post-stress]` header.

Southern rule for **final stressed syllables** (including monosyllables) [iosad2017 p.8, cross-checked
against wiki-cy-phon §Vowels, breit2019 pp.76–78; = `awbery1984` Fig. 1's word-final branch]:

    V → Vː / ˈ_ #                       open final syllable:  tŷ, da
    V → Vː / ˈ_ {b d ɡ v ð f θ χ} #     mab, gradd, rhaff, brath, cath, bach
    V → Vː / ˈ_ {s ʃ ɬ} #               ← SOUTH ONLY (North: short)  glas, nes, llall, pell
    V → V  / ˈ_ {pʰ tʰ kʰ m ŋ} #        twp, clap, cam, trwm, llong, sang
    V → V  / ˈ_ {w j}                   ← SOUTH: always short
    V → V  / ˈ_ CC #                    ← SOUTH: always short  plant, corff, gwallt, Pasg, cosb
    V or Vː / ˈ_ {n l r} #              LEXICALLY DETERMINED — not predictable

The /n l r/ class is genuinely unpredictable and is where the circumflex earns its keep:
*tâl* [ˈtaːl] 'payment' vs *tal* [ˈtal] 'tall'; *tôn* vs *ton*; *gêr* vs *ger*
[breit2019 p.79; iosad2017 pp.7–8]. The same lexical split holds in Southern **penults**: *melyn*
[ˈmɛlːɨn] vs *gelen* [ˈɡɛlɛn]; *celynnen* [kɛˈlɨnːɛn] vs *tenau* [ˈteːnaɨ] [breit2019 p.79].

**/s/ is anomalous: it lengthens a final stressed vowel but shortens a penultimate one** — *bys*
[bɨːs] 'finger' long, *bysedd* [ˈbəsːeð] 'fingers' short [wiki-cy-phon §Vowels]; "in the south,
vowels are obligatorily short before medial [s ʃ ɬ] but long before the same consonants
word-finally" [iosad2017 p.8].

Southern **penults** follow the same rule *except* that they are obligatorily short before medial
/s ʃ ɬ/ [iosad2017 p.8], short before two consonants (*cynta(f)*, *gorffen*), and short before
C + [i̯] (*dynion*, *cofio*) [wiki-cy-phon §Vowels]. The consonants after a short stressed penult
lengthen: *hapus* [ˈhapːʰɨs], *capel* [ˈkapːʰɛl], *tocyn* [ˈtɔkːʰɨn], *cyllell* /ˈkəɬːɛɬ/. In
penults, the first element of **all** diphthongs is short [Morris-Jones 1921:29 via wiki-cy-phon].

**North vs South, side by side:**

| Context | South | North |
|---|---|---|
| stressed penult | contrast retained; long realised **half-long** | **always short** |
| *tonau* / *tonnau* | [ˈtʰoˑnɛ] / [ˈtʰɔnɛ] | identical [ˈtʰɔna] |
| final /s ɬ/ (*pell*, *llall*) | **long** | **short** |
| *heb* | **long** | short |
| /ll/, /s/ + stop (*gwallt, Pasg, cosb*) | **short** | **long** (obligatorily before /sp st sk ɬt ft/) |
| before /w j/ | always short | may be long, lexical |
| /ɬ/ after a stressed vowel | does not geminate; long V before it | geminates; short V before it |
| inventory | no /ɨ ɨː/ | /ɨ ɨː/ retained |

[iosad2017 pp.6–8; wiki-cy-phon §Vowels; breit2019 pp.76–77]

**Consonant length is the other side of the same coin.** "Descriptions of South Welsh agree that
consonants after a short stressed penult are 'half-long'": [ˈtʰɔnˑɛ] *tonnau*, [ˈamˑsɛr] *amser*;
in the stressed ultima *man* [ˈmanː] 'place' vs *mân* [ˈmɑːn] 'fine'; *tal* [ˈtalː] vs *tâl* [ˈtɑːl].
"It appears that the lengthening of consonants after a short stressed vowel in a penultimate
syllable is universal across Welsh dialects" [iosad2017 p.9]. Instrumentally, on C(C)VC
monosyllables (16 speakers, 11 N / 5 S): "coda duration in Modern Welsh C(C)VC monosyllables is a
very good indicator for preceding vowel duration"; vowels are a mean 0.52 of vowel+coda duration;
median lenis-vs-fortis vowel-duration difference ≈ 56 ms; **no significant North/South effect found**
[grawunder2015 §§3.1, 3.3, 3.5, 3.7, 4].

CONFLICT: **is Welsh vowel length fully predictable?**
- Yes [wood1988 pp.233, 236]: the environments are the mechanical residue of historical
  mutation/lenition/spirantisation; `[+SYL] → [+LNG] / [+STR] _ C¹` suffices once geminates are
  underlying (*pen* /pennː/); "the tiresome cataloguing of phonetic environments … will be
  unnecessary."
- No, not fully [iosad2017 pp.8, 12–13]: the /n l r/ class is lexically conditioned and needs
  underlying moraic specification, and loans supply genuinely unpredictable lax long vowels
  ([ˈfɛːnɛst] 'window'; Watkins's [ˈbrɔːn] 'brawn', [ɪndʒɪˈnɛːr] 'engineer'). [breit2019 p.79] sides
  with Iosad: /l n r/ are contrastively moraic.
- Partly reconcilable: Wood describes the *diachronic source* of the environments and does not
  address the /n l r/ lexical split.

CONFLICT: **is length contrastive in Southern penults at all?** [iosad2017 p.7],
[wiki-cy-phon §Vowels], [williams1983 pp.190–191] and [breit2019 p.79] say yes. [liu2018 pp.6–7]
says "vowel length is only contrastive in monosyllabic words" — despite Liu's target being Southern.
**RESOLVED in revision 2 in favour of "yes"** by [awbery1984 pp.69, 72, 74] — see §4.3B. Awbery is
a dedicated phonotactic description of the Southern system with a full environment table and
explicit North/South contrast pairs; `liu2018` is an instrumental study of *stress* whose length
remark is an aside, and whose own data (Table 4 above) is about the stressed/unstressed contrast,
not the penult length contrast. Four sources now say yes against one aside.

CONFLICT: **which coda classes condition length.** [grawunder2015 §3.3]'s data-driven split is
/-k -χ -f -ɬ -m -p -s -t -θ/ (short V) vs /-b -d -ð -v -ɡ -l -n -r/ (long V) — agreeing on /m/ but
treating /l n r/ as uniformly long-taking and putting /s/ and /ɬ/ in the *short* group, contra the
Southern rule above. Sample was 11 N / 5 S pooled.

**Loans and length** — needed by §3:
- Loans "usually retain long vowels in unexpected positions": *gêm* [ˈɡeːm]; the converse *ffres*
  [ˈfrɛs] keeps a short vowel [breit2019 p.78 + n.11].
- Some loans are short where Welsh rules predict long: *bag* /baɡ/, *pin* /pɪn/, *bil* /bɪl/
  [williams1983 p.191].
- Long **lax** vowels appear only in loans: [ˈbrɔːn] 'brawn', [ɪndʒɪˈnɛːr] 'engineer'
  [Watkins 1967 via iosad2017 p.12], [ˈfɛːnɛst] 'window' (south-western) [iosad2017 p.49].
- **"If length is preserved in the loan, it will naturally attract word-stress"**
  [Thomas 1997:72 via buczek2014 p.343].
### 4.4 OPEN — rule ordering: source vowel length vs Welsh stress and length

**This is a decision for the project, not something the sources settle, and the digest deliberately
does not choose.** It is recorded here because §4.1, §4.3, §3.7 and §8.7 each state one side of it
and the contradiction is invisible unless they are read together.

The two source statements, both well attested:

- **(A) Welsh assigns length after stress.** Long vowels occur only under stress [iosad2017 p.7];
  a vowel is long only *while* stressed, so stress shift under suffixation lengthens the new penult
  and shortens the old one — *gair* → *geiriad* → *geiriadur* [breit2019 pp.74, 76]. On this
  reading, input length is **not** carried across: it is discarded, stress is assigned
  penultimately, and Welsh's own length rule (§4.3) recomputes length from the following consonant.
- **(B) Source length is preserved and attracts stress.** "in Welsh the long vowels are
  characteristically found only in accented syllables, thus **if length is preserved in the loan, it
  will naturally attract word-stress**" [Thomas 1997:72 via buczek2014 p.343]. Loans do keep length
  in unexpected places (*gêm* [ˈɡeːm] [breit2019 p.78]) and do end up final-stressed because of it
  (*apêl*, *brigâd*, *cocŵn*, *cysêt* [buczek2014 p.347]); and modern loans that kept a long vowel
  had the **following consonant** lenited to license it (*clog* [wood1988 p.235]).

Both describe real Welsh behaviour. They are not reconcilable as a single deterministic order, so
the tool must pick one and record the choice as a project decision.

| Order | Procedure | Consequence for Irish input |
|---|---|---|
| **L1 — Welsh-first** | discard Irish length → assign penultimate stress → apply §4.3's length rule from the following consonant | Fully deterministic; needs no new machinery. Irish long vowels vanish as a category, so */ɑː/* vs */a/* stops distinguishing outputs. Matches (A) and matches how a fully nativised loan behaves |
| **L2 — length-first** | keep Irish long vowels → place stress on the (rightmost) long vowel → fill in remaining length by §4.3 | Matches (B) and the *apêl/brigâd* class. Produces final-stressed outputs whenever the Irish word ends in a long vowel — which for Irish input is common, so a large fraction of names would stop sounding penultimate-stressed, i.e. stop sounding Welsh |
| **L3 — Welsh-first with consonant repair** | assign penultimate stress → **if** an Irish long vowel lands in a stressed syllable, lenite the following consonant to license it (§8.7); otherwise shorten | Preserves length where Welsh can, drops it where it cannot. Closest to the attested modern-loan pattern (*clog*), but that pattern is "usually" over a handful of examples (§8.7), and it has no answer for a long vowel in a **closed penult**, where Welsh has no attested repair at all |
| **L4 — nativisation-grade switch** | apply L2 to a marked "foreign-flavoured" subset, L1 to the rest | Has direct source support — "Older loans tend to be fully assimilated, while more recent … loanwords normally retain their (nearly) English pronunciation" [buczek2014 p.339], and morphological integration regularises stress unconditionally [buczek2014 p.347]. Costs a per-word diacritic the generator must set |

Knock-on effects to check whichever is chosen: (i) `/ə/` is barred from the final syllable
[breit2019 p.60], so any order that leaves a reduced vowel there needs a repair; (ii) adding an
epithet suffix re-runs stress and therefore re-runs length (§4.1); (iii) if L2 or L4 is chosen, the
romanizer must emit the circumflex, since that is how Welsh writes exactly this situation
[wiki-cy-orth §Diacritics].

### The w/y ambiguities (flagged per brief)

- **⟨w⟩ is a vowel /ʊ uː/ or a consonant /w/**, and the spelling does not distinguish them:
  *gwraidd* /ˈɡwraið/ 'root' (one syllable, consonantal w) vs *gẃraidd* /ˈɡʊ.raið/ 'manly'
  (two syllables, vocalic w) — the acute is available to force the vowel reading but is
  "very often omitted in casual writing" [wiki-cy-orth §Diacritics].
- **⟨wy⟩ is sometimes the diphthong /ʊi/ and sometimes consonantal /w/ + vowel**: *gwyn*
  /ɡwɪn/ (S), *gwych* /ɡwiːχ/ (S) have consonantal w [wiki-cy-orth §Diphthongs n.3].
- **⟨y⟩ has two values by position**: /ə/ in unstressed monosyllables (*y*, *fy*) and in all
  **non-final** syllables regardless of stress; /ɪ iː/ (South) in **word-final** syllables
  regardless of stress. *ynys* 'island' = /ˈənɪs/ (S) [wiki-cy-orth n.5]. This is a genuine
  positional rule the generator must implement, not an ambiguity to resolve per word.
- **⟨yw⟩** is the diphthong /ɪu/ (S) only when not followed by a vowel; in *cywir*, *tywod*
  it is /ə/ + consonantal /w/ [wiki-cy-orth §Diphthongs n.4].
- **⟨i⟩ before a vowel** is /j/, and ⟨si di ti⟩ before a vowel are /ʃ dʒ tʃ/ — so ⟨si⟩ is
  ambiguous between /ʃ/ and /s/+/j/. The diaeresis disambiguates /ij/: *copïo* /kɔˈpi.jɔ/
  [wiki-cy-orth n.1, §Diacritics].
- South Wales distinguishes the letter names *i-dot* and *u-bedol* precisely because ⟨i⟩ and
  ⟨u⟩ are pronounced identically in the South [wiki-cy-orth §opening].

### Diacritics
Circumflex ⟨â ê î ô û ŵ ŷ⟩ = always long (but not all long vowels carry it). Grave = short
where long is expected (*pàs* /pas/ vs *pas* /paːs/), used mainly in loans. Acute = final
stress (*gwacáu*, *dicléin*, *jiráff*). Diaeresis = vowels in hiatus. Grave, acute and
diaeresis are "very often omitted"; the circumflex is usually kept [wiki-cy-orth §Diacritics].

### Predicting length from the spelling
A vowel with no diacritic is long only if stressed, and then: in a final open syllable
(*da* /dɑː/); before ⟨b d g⟩ and before all fricatives except ⟨ll⟩ (*mab* /mɑːb/, *hoff*
/hoːf/, *peth* /peːθ/, *nos* /noːs/). Short: unstressed; before ⟨p t c⟩ (*iet* /jɛt/, *lloc*
/ɬɔk/); before all consonant clusters except those starting with s or ll (*sant* /sant/,
*perth* /pɛrθ/, *Ebrill* /ˈɛbrɪɬ/). Before ⟨l m n r⟩ length is **lexically unpredictable**:
*gwin* /ɡwiːn/ vs *prin* /prɪn/, *hen* /heːn/ vs *pen* /pɛn/ [wiki-cy-orth §Predicting vowel
length from orthography]. For generated names this is a free choice, marked with a circumflex
if long.

### Practice for foreign names
Three competing conventions coexist on Welsh Wikipedia [cy-wiki-arddull §Sillafiad enwau
gwledydd]: (a) *Atlas Cymraeg Newydd* keeps the native/international spelling unless a
familiar Welsh name exists (*Kuwait*, but *yr Aifft*); (b) *Geiriadur yr Academi* respells
by Welsh letter values (*Ffiji* not *Fiji*); (c) follow the Bwrdd yr Iaith list. Unfamiliar
exonyms are glossed on first use: *Napoli (Naples)*, *Efrog (York)*. For this project (b) is
the relevant model. The Welsh Language Commissioner's *Enwau lleoedd tramor* page turned out
to contain **no name list at all** — it points at BydTermCymru, which is 403-blocked
[cyg-enwau §Enwau lleoedd tramor].

| **article *yr / y / 'r*** | definite; no indefinite article | *yr* before vowel or h-; *y* before consonant; *'r* after a vowel | *yr afon* 'the river', *y tŷ* 'the house', *o'r tŷ* 'from the house'; ⟨w-⟩ counts as a consonant (*y waedd*) but ⟨i-⟩ as a vowel (*yr iaith*), and word-initial *wy-* takes *yr* (*yr wyneb*) [morrisjones1913 pp.192–3 §114] |

Notes for epithet building:
- **-og is the direct cognate of Irish -ach** [morrisjones1913 p.256 §153(5)], so an Irish
  epithet in *-ach* has an obvious Welsh-side counterpart; this is a design opportunity, not a
  source claim about adaptation.
- The **soft mutation** is the productive sandhi that follows the article on feminine nouns
  and follows most adjectival/genitival attachment. But: in the spoken corpus **the names of
  individuals do not mutate** — proper names supply a zero-mutation context
  [bmj-mutations p.21]. That matters directly for a name generator.
- English-origin loans are irregular in the mutation system: *garej, ga:t, ge:m, glasses,
  good, greedy* are exempt from the g-dropping soft mutation [bmj-mutations p.21]; and a
  small set of loans keep a voiced initial [l] rather than being fitted to the *ll-/l-*
  alternation (*lamp, larum, letys, lwans*) [wood1988 p.231]. The usual accommodation is to
  enter the loan as an **unmutated radical** [wood1988 p.231].

## 8. Irish-specific mismatch notes

**Read the section header first: there is no Irish→Welsh adaptation evidence in the open corpus.**
`wikt-cy-from-gle` — the only Irish-to-Welsh word list found anywhere open — is a bare category
membership list: no etymons, no dates beyond the category label, no phonology
[wikt-cy-from-gle, whole file]. `morrisjones1913` mentions exactly one Irish loan in passing
(*brat* 'apron' [morrisjones1913 §51 ii]) and one loan the other way (Ir. *claideb* < W.
[morrisjones1913 §110 ii]). **Every conclusion below is therefore drawn from how Welsh treats the
same phenomenon in loans from English or Latin, or from Welsh's own internal history.** Where that
is Old/Middle-Welsh sound change rather than loan adaptation, it is said so explicitly.

CONFLICT: two of the Wiktionary list's headwords are treated by Morris Jones as **inherited
cognates, not loans** — *chwedl* ("Ar. sq- gives Ir. sc-, W. chw-: Ir. *scél*, W. *chwedl*") and
*ochr* ("W. *ochr* beside Ir. *ochar*") [morrisjones1913 §§ at lines 9521, 10767 of the OCR; page
headers not recoverable]. This thins an already thin corpus.

### 8.1 Broad (velarized) vs slender (palatalized) consonants

**Welsh has no Cˠ/Cʲ contrast** [wiki-cy-phon §Consonants; williams1995 p.1]. Its one
secondary-articulation series is **labial**, not palatal: /lʷ nʷ rʷ/ "used in restricted contexts"
[williams1995 p.1].

**Precedent: partial — strong for palatal *segments*, none for a palatalization *contrast*.**

**There are two layers here and they give opposite answers. A modern generator must not be handed
the historical one.**

CONFLICT (period, not dialect): the ME/early-NE layer sends foreign palatals to /ʃ/; the modern
layer keeps them as /tʃ dʒ/. Both are well attested. Neither is chosen here.

**LAYER 1 — ME/early NE *(historical evidence only; do NOT encode as a modern rule)*.** In this
layer, **initial** /tʃ/ and /dʒ/ generally went to ⟨si⟩ = /ʃ/:
- E /tʃ/ → ⟨si⟩, the stop dropped: "There is apparently no trace of the dental stop which exists in
  the E pronunciation" — *sialc* 'chalk', *siambr* 'chamber', *siawns* 'chance', *simnai* 'chimney'
  [parry1923 pp.225–226 §87] *(ME/early NE)*.
- E /dʒ/ → same, with one recorded exception (*dsiet* 'jet'): *Siac* 'Jack', *siaced* 'jacket',
  *Sion* 'John', *sinsir* 'ginger' [parry1923 pp.226–227 §88] *(ME/early NE)*.
- **Position matters even inside this layer**: final /dʒ/ went to /-s/, /-ts/ or /-ds/, not /ʃ/ —
  *badge* > *baeds*, *page* > *paits*, *college* > *colas* [parry1923 p.245 §122] *(ME/early NE)*;
  and later /tʃ/ loans show the dental: *maits*, *waets*, *twyts* [parry1923 pp.244–245 §121]
  *(late/NE)*. So even "every initial palatal → /ʃ/" is a generalisation over one position in one
  period.

**LAYER 2 — modern *(this is the treatment a modern generator should be looking at)*.** /tʃ/ and
/dʒ/ are **retained**, and in Southern Welsh /dʒ/ additionally has a **native** source:
- "/tʃ, dʒ/ … have also become established in some dialects, **especially in South Welsh**, where
  they are the result of palatalisation of /t, d/ preceding /i/, e.g. *'sgidiau* [sɡ̊ɪdʒɛ] 'shoes'"
  [breit2019 p.62] **(South)** — i.e. in the target variety /dʒ/ is not even loan-restricted.
- Modern loans keep the affricate and spell it ⟨j⟩ or ⟨ts⟩: *garej*, *jiráff*, *ffrij*, *jeli*
  [ˈdʒɛlɪ], *jîns*, *tsips*, *wats* [wiki-cy-orth §Non-native letters, §Letter names, n.1;
  breit2019 pp.65–66].
- The ⟨si⟩ device nevertheless remains live for /ʃ/: "The sequence *si* indicates /ʃ/ when followed
  by a vowel; similarly, *di* and *ti* sometimes indicate /dʒ/ and /tʃ/ respectively when followed
  by a vowel" [wiki-cy-orth n.1]; implemented for South Welsh, "sI" → /ʃ/ [williams1995 p.2].

**The orthographic device is period-independent and is the transferable part.** ⟨si⟩'s value is
stated explicitly: "The si (for sh) found in W has the value of E sh **or that of s + i
(consonantal)**" [parry1923 p.224 §86] — i.e. **the same digraph covers both /ʃ/ and /sj/. Welsh
already has an orthographic device for "consonant + palatal onglide", and it survives into the
modern orthography** [wiki-cy-orth n.1].

**But where the donor had /Cj/ and no palatal, Welsh kept plain C + /ɪu/ and created nothing:**
*nature* > *natur*, *adventure* > *antur*, *question* > *cwestiwn*, *picture* > *pictiwr*, *steward*
> *ystiwart* — the English /tj/ > /tʃ/ change "is not reflected in W … presumably because they were
borrowed prior to the E change" [parry1923 p.238 §§107–108; p.223 §84]. Contrast *-tion* > *-siwn*
(*nasiwn, pensiwn, sesiwn*), which *were* already [ʃ] in English [parry1923 p.237 §105].
**So the rule is: Welsh copies the donor's surface segment. It does not synthesise palatalization
from a /Cj/ input.**

**The one attested Welsh device for a bare palatal onglide on a velar** is Parry-Williams §81a:
`∅ → j / #{ɡ,k} _ V[+front]` — *guard* > *giard*, *gate* > *giêt*, *gaffer* > *giaffar*, *guide* >
*gieid*. Crucially it then generalised inside Welsh: "in the Carns. dial. … this glide is heard in W
words as spoken by the older people, e.g. in *ciartra* for *cartref*, *ciath* for *cath*, **even
before non-front vowels**" [parry1923 p.221 §81a]. **Caveat: this is Caernarfonshire, i.e. North
Welsh, and obsolescent.**

**Welsh's own live /s/-palatalization is Southern and triggered from the LEFT**: "In northern
accents, it can occur when /s/ precedes /iː j/… In some **southern** accents it is produced when /s/
**follows** /ɪ/ or /iː/, e.g. *mis* [miːʃ] 'month'" [wiki-cy-phon §Fricatives]; *sut* → [ʃʊd] in the
South vs [sɨt] in the North [wiki-cy-lang §Dialects]. **This is the nearest thing in Welsh to a
slender-coda effect, and it is Southern.**

**i-affection is not a colouring mechanism.** It "takes place when the suffix begins with /i, ɨ/ or
/j/" and "mostly concern[s] the vowel /a/ or diphthongs beginning with /a/": *truan* → *trueni*,
*gwaedd* → *gweiddi*, *cawr* → *cewri*, *mab* → *meibion* [wiki-cy-phon §Vowel mutations]. No source
describes it as affecting consonants; it is morphologically conditioned and largely lexicalised.

**Options** (no recommendation):
1. Depalatalize/develarize wholesale: Cʲ → C, Cˠ → C. Loses all information; matches Welsh's actual
   inventory.
2. Cʲ → C + /j/ before a vowel (written ⟨Ci⟩, which is exactly Welsh's own convention), dropped in
   codas. Cˠ → C.
3. Route only the coronal-ish slender consonants to Welsh palatals per [parry1923 §§86–88]:
   sʲ → /ʃ/, tʲ → /tʃ/, dʲ → /dʒ/, and depalatalize the rest.
4. As (2), plus the ⟨Ci-/Gi-⟩ device of [parry1923 p.221 §81a] for velars specifically — accepting
   that it is a North Welsh dialectism.

### 8.2 Irish /ɣ/

**Precedent: no** for Irish /ɣ/ specifically. Strong target-internal precedent for what Welsh did
with the /ɣ/ it once had — **but that is Old-to-Middle-Welsh sound change, not loan adaptation.**

- The segment was already gone by Middle Welsh: "In Ml. W. the letter g stands initially and
  medially for the sound g. **The voiced spirant ȝ had then disappeared**"
  [morrisjones1913 §19 iv(1) p.23].
- **Initially → ∅**: "The soft mutation of g has uniformly disappeared as an initial sound."
  **Medially → ∅ or /j/** "before a vowel, or before l, r or n" [morrisjones1913 §110 ii(1)].
- After a liquid it surfaces as /j/; word-finally it either assimilates (North) or **vocalises to
  /a/ (South)**: Ml.W. *daly* → N.W. *dâl*, **S.W. *dala***; Ml.W. *boly* → N.W. *bol*, **S.W.
  *bola*** [morrisjones1913 §110 ii(2)].
- Post-tonic after /a/ it simply vanished: Brit.-Lat. *Maglagni, Corbagni, Broccagni* > W. *Maelan,
  Corfan, Brychan* [morrisjones1913 §104 p.165].
- Latin loans confirm: intervocalic Lat. /g/ → /j/ or ∅ — *signum* > *swyn*, *corrigia* > *carrai*,
  *largus* > *llary* [morrisjones1913 §104(2) p.165].

**Options:** (1) /ɣ/ → ∅ (Welsh's initial and post-tonic outcome). (2) /ɣ/ → /j/ (Welsh's medial and
post-liquid outcome), ∅ initially. (3) /ɣ/ → /ɡ/ (restore the unlenited stop; no Welsh precedent but
matches Welsh's own ⟨g⟩ spelling of the radical). (4) /ɣ/ → /χ/ (nearest surviving dorsal fricative,
sacrificing voicing; no precedent).

### 8.3 Irish /x/ and /ç/ vs Welsh ⟨ch⟩

**Precedent: partial** — /x/ has an obvious landing site; /ç/ has none.

Irish /x/ maps directly onto Welsh ⟨ch⟩, and the velar/uvular difference is sub-phonemic in Welsh.
⟨ch⟩ occurs word-initially, medially and finally [jipa-north p.494], arises as the aspirate mutation
of /k/, and is commonly followed by /w/ (*chwech*). Initial /χw/ is realised **[ʍ] in the South**:
*chwech* → [ʍeːχ] [wiki-cy-phon §Consonants].

CONFLICT (repeated from §1, because it is load-bearing here): is Southern ⟨ch⟩ [x] or [χ]?
- [wiki-cy-phon §Fricatives]: uvular generally, "velar variant [x]" only for **some southwestern**
  speakers.
- [jipa-north p.494] reports the literature as "uvular /χ/ in Northern Welsh, and as velar /x/ in
  Southern Welsh (Ball & Williams 2001)" — then transcribes its own **Northern** speaker as velar.
- [breit2019 pp.61–62]: "usually /x/ in South Welsh and /χ/ in North Welsh", but two sentences later
  "Most dialects have uvular [χ]".
- [williams1995 p.1], the only direct Southern transcription in the corpus, prints **/χ/**.
The PHOIBLE row has `χ`. **If Southern ⟨ch⟩ is velar [x], Irish /x/ is an exact match; if uvular, a
near one. "Either way no repair is needed" is a `(design inference)` from inventory fit — no source
observes Irish /x/ being adapted into Welsh, and this corpus contains no observation of foreign /x/
adaptation at all except the reverse case (English ⟨gh⟩ /x/ **retained** as Welsh ⟨ch⟩ in early
loans: *dracht*, *fflicht*, *slachtar* [parry1923 pp.248–249 §128] *(ME/early NE)*). The
romanization is ⟨ch⟩ on either reading.**

**Irish /ç/: no coverage whatsoever.** No source in the directory lists [ç] in a Welsh inventory. The
only palatal fricative Welsh has is /ʃ/.
**Options:** (1) /ç/ → /χ/ (merge with broad /x/, consistent with depalatalizing everywhere else).
(2) /ç/ → /ʃ/ (follows the Welsh loan habit of routing foreign palatals to /ʃ/,
[parry1923 §§86–88]). (3) /ç/ → /χj/ or /hj/ (preserves palatality; neither sequence is attested in
Welsh). (4) /ç/ → /h/ (unattested).

### 8.4 Irish /h/

**Precedent: yes** that Welsh has /h/ and borrows it; **partial** for non-initial positions, where
Welsh bars it.

Welsh /h/ is charted in every inventory including the Southern one [williams1995 p.1;
wiki-cy-phon §Consonants; jipa-north p.489]. **Its distribution is the problem:**
- "/h/ is usually found only **word-initially or at the beginning of a stressed syllable**;
  **after a stress it generally disappears**, e.g. *brenhines* 'queen' but *brénin* 'king'.
  Consequently **/h/ is not found finally in any word**" [wiki-cy-phon §Fricatives]; "all but /h/
  may appear word-finally" [jipa-north p.494].
- Post-stress /h/ does not simply delete — it **provects** the following consonant: *bwyd* + *-ha* >
  *bwyta* [bʊɨtːa]; *gwlyb* + *-hach* > *glypach* [ɡlɘpːaχ] [wiki-cy-phon §Consonant mutations].
- Loan precedent: English words vacillating on /h/ were borrowed **without** it — *oribl*, *ostes*,
  *ostler*, *Ilar* 'Hilary' [parry1923 p.230 §92].

**Which varieties lose /h/ entirely?** The corpus contains exactly **one** statement: "In parts of
**south-east Wales**, /h/ disappears in all positions, and in these same dialects **/r̥/ is replaced
by /r/**" [wiki-cy-phon §Fricatives]. No other source mentions h-dropping. **Llanwrtyd is never
named in any source file.** By dialect map it falls in Powyseg, not Gwenhwyseg, so it is outside the
h-dropping zone — but that is an inference `(unattested for Llanwrtyd itself)`. See §9, item 1.

**Options** (for Irish /h/ outside the licensed positions): (1) delete it (matches *brenin*).
(2) delete it and provect/geminate the adjacent consonant (matches *bwyta*, *glypach*). (3) map
non-initial /h/ to /χ/ (the fricative Welsh does license word-finally in that region). (4) retain it
regardless of position, accepting a configuration no Welsh word has.

### 8.5 Voiceless sonorants

**Precedent: yes — and better than a match.** Welsh not only has them, it has a documented history
of *adding* them to loans that lacked them.

Inventory: /m̥ n̥ ŋ̥ r̥/ plus /ɬ/, all listed for **South Welsh** with no RP equivalent
[williams1995 p.1 Table 1; wiki-cy-phon §Consonants; jipa-north p.489].

Distribution:
- **/m̥ n̥ ŋ̥/ occur mostly word-initially, as a consequence of nasal mutation**, and are "often
  pronounced as" /m n ŋ/ + /h/ [wiki-cy-phon §Consonants]. They are "generally followed by a full,
  distinctly enunciated /h/": *fy mhen* [ə ˈm̥hɛn], *yng Nghaerdydd* [əŋ ŋ̥haɨrˈdɨːð]; in *fy mhres*
  the /h/ lands after the /r/: [ə ˈm̥r̥heːs] [wiki-cy-phon §Other consonants].
- **/r̥/ is confined to initial or pre-tonic syllables** and never word-final
  [jipa-north p.493 **(North)**; wiki-cy-phon §Fortis and lenis].
- **/ɬ/ is the least restricted**: it "may appear in **any** position in the word"
  [jipa-north p.494]. It is a genuine fricative — energy at 4–6 kHz, high centre of gravity, no
  clear spectral peak, medium amplitude alongside /χ/ [jipa-north p.494].

**Does Southern Welsh keep /r̥/?** Yes. `williams1995`'s explicit South inventory includes it
[williams1995 p.1]; the loss is confined to the south-east, where /h/ also goes
[wiki-cy-phon §Fricatives].

**Is /ɬ/ just a devoiced /l/? No — and Welsh has a *separate* [l̥].** "Welsh does seem to have a
phonetically voiceless variant of /l/ which is supposedly acoustically distinct from /ɬ/; this is
essentially restricted to those positions in onset clusters where no voicing contrast can be encoded
(e.g. /tl/ → [tl̥]). This is unproblematic since **/ɬ/ cannot appear in that position**, so the two
are never in contrastive distribution" [breit2019 p.73]. Glides devoice the same way after a
voiceless consonant: *chwerthin*, *clociau* [wiki-cy-phon §Diphthongs].

**The loan precedent for creating them** *(ME/early NE; explicitly receding by the late layer)*:
"Initial *l* and *r*, being now foreign to W as radical (unmutated) forms, were changed initially in
W very often into **ll** … and **rh**"
[parry1923 pp.230–231 §95]. **But** this is optional and the later the loan the less likely: *lawnt,
lili, lamp, larder, recorder, redi, rwbel* keep plain l-/r- [parry1923 §95], and modern *lamp,
larum, letys, lwans* keep voiced [l] [wood1988 p.231]. **So this is a design choice with attestation
on both sides**, and it is one of the few places where Welsh will make an Irish name look
distinctively Welsh at a glance.

**The s+C prothesis rule: see §3.1, which states scope, medium and conditioning precisely and lays
out the three self-consistent encodings (P / S / C).** The short form, for the Irish mapping:

- The **scope** differs by source. Morris Jones states it over **s + any consonant**: "**Initial s

  'school'" — native, not borrowed from Latin *iscola*, "since Corn., Bret., Ir. *scol* do not show

  loan-adaptation statement covers **s + stop only** [parry1923 p.223 §84] *(ME/early NE)*. So
  Irish /sp st sk/ fall under both; **Irish /sm sn sl sr/ fall only under the wider native rule**,
  for which there is no loan evidence at all.
- It is **not obligatory in speech** in either source: "not heard except in words in which it is
  accented, as *ysgol, ystrad, ysbryd* … but *sgubor, strodur, sgrifen, stryd*"
  [morrisjones1913 §23 ii p.26]; "hardly ever pronounced unless the accent falls on it"
  [parry1923 p.223 §84]. In Old Welsh it was not written at all and did not count as a syllable in
  the oldest verse [morrisjones1913 §23 ii p.26].
- **`(unattested inference)`** — the digest author's derivation, not a source statement: since
  prothesis on a disyllabic /sC/-initial input yields a trisyllable whose penultimate stress lands
  on the epenthetic vowel, the stress condition would be self-satisfying for exactly those inputs,
  and not for monosyllables. No source derives this. **A decision point, not a settled rule.**

Note the orthographic consequence: /sp sk/ are spelled ⟨sb sg⟩ in Welsh because /b d ɡ/ are devoiced
in that context [wiki-cy-phon §Stops].

Welsh's own permitted cluster-initial consonants, per the Southern letter-to-sound system: /m n ŋ
m̥ n̥ ŋ̥ χ θ b d ɡ v ð f p t k s/ [williams1995 p.3] — note the nasals and voiceless nasals as C1
(from mutation, *mh-*, *ngh-* + r/l) and the fricatives /χ θ f ð v s/.

**Options** (for /mn/ only, the one gap): (1) → /n/. (2) → /m/ + copy vowel + /n/, extending the
*pobl* → [ˈpɔbɔl] repair to the onset. (3) permit *mn-* by analogy with /kn ɡn/. (4) prothesise
*ymn-* by analogy with s+C (no attestation for a non-/s/ first member).

### 8.7 Vowel length and the penultimate-stress interaction

**Precedent: yes for a long vowel in a FINAL syllable; no for a long vowel in a closed penult.**

Welsh's constraint set, from §4: long vowels occur only under stress; stress is penultimate; and in
the final stressed syllable length is determined by the following consonant. So an Irish long vowel
arriving in a Welsh word is subject to three filters at once — **is it in the stressed syllable? is
that syllable the penult or the ultima? and is the following consonant in the lengthening set?**

**The attested repair is to change the CONSONANT, not the vowel.** Wood states the rule as
`[+SYL] → [+LNG] / [+STR] _ C¹` over the set /l n r b d ɡ v ð f θ s χ/ [wood1988 pp.233–234], and
then:
- Loans with a short vowel keep a **geminate** final stop: *map* [mapː], *siop* [ʃɔpː], *mat*
  [matː], *cloc* [ˈklɔkː] [wood1988 p.235].
- **"For loanwords where an original long vowel has been retained, the final consonant is usually
  lenited and then accords with (12)": *cloak* → *clog* [ˈkloːɡ]** [wood1988 p.235].
- Independently: "E. tenuis after a long vowel becomes a media, as W. *clog* < E. *cloak*, W. *grâd*
  < E. *groat*, re-borrowed as *grât*" [morrisjones1913 §51 ii].
- Wood's own gloss: "the productivity of this rule is illustrated by the way in which this
  environment is preserved when the syllable structure of loanwords is adapted to it"
  [wood1988 p.235].

**So the precedent is: in modern loans that kept a source long vowel, the final consonant is
*usually* lenited into the lengthening set.** Note carefully what that is and is not:
Wood's wording is "**usually**", the corpus behind it is small (*clog* is his only worked example,
with `morrisjones1913 §51 ii` independently adding *grâd* < *groat*), and it is a statement about
**English loans into Welsh**, not an Irish→Welsh rule. It is the best-supported pattern in this
section, but it is **a precedent, not a categorical mapping** `(unattested for Irish)`. The
optionality word must survive into the rule file: an Irish long vowel in a final stressed syllable
*may* be preserved by leniting the following consonant; it is not guaranteed to be, and Wood
records the competing outcome (short vowel + geminate final) in the same passage.

A long vowel in a **closed penult** has no attested repair: North Welsh is categorically short there,
and the South at best half-long [iosad2017 pp.7–8; wiki-cy-phon §Vowels].
And the length rule interacts with stress shift: a vowel is long *only while stressed*, so adding a
Welsh suffix (an epithet ending, a plural) will **shorten a previously long final vowel and lengthen
the new penult** [breit2019 pp.74, 76].
One more fact that cuts the other way: "**in Welsh the long vowels are characteristically found only
in accented syllables, thus if length is preserved in the loan, it will naturally attract
word-stress**" [Thomas 1997:72 via buczek2014 p.343] — i.e. an Irish long vowel is itself a reason
to put the stress there, which is how loans like *apêl*, *brigâd*, *cocŵn* end up final-stressed.

**Options** (closed penult): (1) shorten (the North outcome). (2) realise half-long, using the
Southern *tonau/defaid* pattern. (3) lenite/degeminate the following consonant to license length —
unattested medially. (4) shift stress to the syllable carrying the Irish long vowel, exploiting
Welsh's tolerance of final stress in loans (§3.7 class 3) and Thomas's length-attracts-stress
generalisation.

the Southern *dala/bola* vocalisation [morrisjones1913 §110 ii(2)] — unattested for loan diphthongs.

### 8.9 Initial mutations and genitives on the Irish side

The sources say little about how Welsh handles donor-side alternations, but three facts bear on it:

1. **Loans enter as unmutated radicals.** "the usual way of accommodating loanwords into Welsh"
   [wood1988 p.231]. **Read this precisely: it says a loan is fitted into the *Welsh* mutation
   system by being assigned an unmutated *Welsh* radical. It says nothing about reversing
   morphological alternations in the donor language.**
   `(design decision, not a Welsh adaptation fact)` — whether Irish input should be normalised to
   its radical (un-lenited, nominative) *before* it reaches the Welsh rules is a **source-side**
   choice and belongs to the Irish digest, not here. No source in this corpus licenses it. It is
   noted only because the Welsh-side fact is easy to over-read into it.
2. **A vowel- or w- or l-/r-initial loan gets *reanalysed* as if it were already mutated**, and a
   new radical is back-formed: *honest* > *gonest*, *welt* > *gwalt*, *grand* > *crand*, *banner* >
   *maner* [parry1923 §§78, 81, 89, 93–95]. **Irish lenition outputs (/v w j h ɣ x/) are exactly the
   kind of input that would trigger this**, though no source says so.
3. **Personal names do not mutate.** In the spoken corpus "the names of individuals do not mutate
   and this is viewed as zero mutation" [bmj-mutations p.21] — with a handful of exceptions for
   fictional characters and brands (*Fister Blaidd*, *Fatman*, *Fackdonalds*). **Place names,
   by contrast, do mutate** [bmj-mutations p.21]. For a name generator this means the output should
   normally be a radical form.
